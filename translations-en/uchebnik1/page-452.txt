Chosroes, the Persian king, broke the peace, and the Avars ravaged Thrace, and both Greek armies were destroyed and annihilated, because the impious and vile one made himself more honorable than the lawful and pious one.

To this were added a pestilence among men and beasts, and great barrenness of the land, and a severe winter, so that the sea froze and many fish died.

This evil Phocas, after all his countless evil sins, added this final deed: he held horse races. The Greens could not endure the insult and said to him: "You have drunk the cup again." The godless traitor was angered at them, and many he mutilated—that is, cut off their noses—and many he mocked and disfigured, and hung their limbs on columns, and to many he cut off their heads, while others he sewed into sacks and threw into the sea.

Phocas ordered the eparch to shut many other people in prison, so that within a few days he might put them to death.

The people of the Greens' faction gathered and set fire to the prison, and all the prisoners came out and fled.

Then wrath overtook the head of the impious Phocas, because he had wrought countless evil. Heraclius, general of Africa, prepared many ships, gathered from Africa and Mauretania, and came to Constantinople, bearing with him the Icon of the Lord Not Made by Hands, as George of Pisidia declares. Both armies fought, and Heraclius by the grace of Christ defeated the evildoer Phocas. When he had been defeated, Photinus, one of the nobles, who had earlier plotted to overthrow Phocas during the horse races, suddenly entered the chamber of the wretched Phocas with many soldiers and dishonorably cast him down from the throne, stripped off the imperial garments and clothed him in a black cloak, and placed fetters, that is chains, on his neck, and brought the wretched one dishonorably to Heraclius like a vagabond.

Heraclius saw Phocas and said to him: "Is this how, wretched one, you governed the life of the country?" First Heraclius ordered Phocas's hands and feet cut off, then had strips cut from his shoulders, and his shameful parts cut off and hung on a pole, because of his countless outrages that he had committed.

Finally his head was cut off with a sword. The rest of the body of the evilly named one was dragged to the Forum of the Ox, that is, the Square of the Bull, and given over to fire and burned.

The Tale of the Monk. There was a certain monk in Constantinople, a holy man who had great boldness before God; he murmured against God and spoke thus concerning Phocas: "Lord, why have You set such an evil ruler over us?" And because this monk spoke thus many times, a Voice came to him from God, saying: "Because I have not found another worse than he."
