And on the next day the Byzantines and Romans joined battle, and the Romans defeated the Byzantines with all their strength, and captured Byzantium.

There were 5,500 years from the Creation of the world to the Incarnation of Christ, and the pious Tsar Constantine the Great began to reign in old Rome from the carnal Coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, in the one hundred eighteenth (118) and two hundred second (202) year, that is, in the 320th year (the year of the victory over Maxentius; from the Creation of the world, the 5820th), being emperor from the beginning of the rule of the Augusti of the Roman Empire.

On the Founding of Constantine’s City. While reigning, the thrice-blessed Constantine was moved by divine command and wished to found a city in the Western lands in honor of his name—on the plain opposite the Sun (Ilium), above the tomb of Aias (Ajax), where, according to accounts, was the anchorage of the ships of the Greeks who warred with the Trojans. But God in a dream commanded him to build in Byzantium.

And soon, having surveyed the indicated place and gone all around it, and having observed that it had a convenient situation, air fragrant with herbs, and places raised on high which by their position afforded a view of the distant surroundings, and having made a comparable assessment, he did as he had been instructed in the vision: he founded the God-protected city and named it in honor of his own name, Constantine’s City, in the eastern vicinity of the Sun (Ilium). He also built a beautiful palace descending to the sea, and a hippodrome for horses, and constructed two large covered passages. And if anyone wished to appraise the security and wisdom of the buildings, he would call them almost a second creation of Phidias; he created them to his own honor.

And leaving the Western lands, he came to Byzantium with his mother Helena and his son Constantius, and brought noble and renowned inhabitants from Rome, and having gathered a fair number from other places, and having granted them great houses, he settled them to live in the city.

And he renewed the city of Byzantium, founded in antiquity by Byzas, king of the Thracians, and extended its walls by one league, and named it Constantinople.

He commanded that this same Constantine’s City be honored as New Rome. And he set up a stone column of a single stone, all purple and worthy of every praise, which he had brought from Rome, in a place called the Linen Market. And on it he placed a bronze statue—the image of a man, which he brought from the Sun City (Ilium) in the land of the Phrygians, having seven rays on its head. This column was transported by sea for three years, since it was large and heavy; from the sea to the Market it was transported in a year.
