And he threw down many bronze idols for the needs of the aforesaid church, and much marble and psiphidia, that is, crushed marble, and marble columns he took from many churches and buildings for the construction of that church.

He also removed the bronze statue that stood in the senate; it depicted a bishop holding a staff entwined by a snake.

Having taken it down, they set it up in the imperial courtyard. The emperor, coming there and standing by the statue, put his finger into the mouth of the bronze snake. But within that snake was a live snake, and it bit him, and he barely recovered, having taken much against that poison.

And the statue of Solomon stood in the so-called imperial place in the basilica, which he removed, and, having made his own image, placed it in the foundation of the New Church, as if making himself a sacrifice to God the Word.

He set out again to war against Melitene, and waged many battles with the Agarenes, and took many captives, and returned to Constantinople.

And holy Ignatius escaped from the storm of life and reached good and calm harbors, departing to the Lord. The body of this patriarch was placed in the monastery in the settlement of Zatourion, which he himself had founded.

The deep-thinking Photius, constantly thirsting for the throne and striving to take the church again, craftily devised the following. Having taken the beginnings of the names of the imperial sons and the empress, that is, one or two letters from each name, he composed a riddle from them and explained it to the wise men of that time, concealing the origin of the riddle. And because of this the emperor acknowledged him as great in wisdom.

And therefore the emperor again raised him to the patriarchate, and again the most zealous one accepted the throne. But the mind of all born on earth is prone to sin, and there are in people certain habits of nature and age.

The emperor set out to war against Germanicia, and having captured it, returned to Constantinople.

Constantine, who was born to Emperor Michael by Eudokia, in a word, the son of Basil, came to the end of his life, over whom Basil grieved endlessly, because he loved him greatly.
