The Romans began to build the embankment on the twelfth day of the month Artemisius, that is, May, and finished it with great toil on the nineteenth day, and set up the battering engines.

John, that is, Johanan, made a tunnel from the city and reached the embankment underground, and laid dry wood with pitch and sulfur, and set it alight, and returned. The wood caught fire, the earth softened, and the embankments collapsed with a great crash, and with them the towers. At first smoke came out with dust, and the flame could not blaze because it was covered. But when the earth softened and began to cave in, the flame blazed up. The Romans were terrified, seeing the sudden fire from the earth, and they were greatly dismayed. Two days later, sallies with fire were made against the other embankments as well. Megassar of Adiabene and Hageir, which means the Lame, were at that time braver than all among the Jews. As if to a wedding, they went into battle, and fought without delay, without fear, and passed through the midst of the enemies, and set fire to the embankment and the engines of war. They fought them with spears and swords and struggled hand to hand, but they did not retreat until the engines caught fire. The Romans tried to save the engines from the fire. The Jews hindered them from the walls, and some ran out and fought, sparing not their bodies, against those who were extinguishing the fire. The Romans dragged the engines out of the flames, while the Jews leaped through the flames, seized the hot iron, and took the engines away from the Romans. The Romans, surrounded by flame, despaired of setting things right and thought only of how to escape.

But the Jews kept coming, encouraged by their victory, and did not guard themselves, and did not duck.

The Jews even reached the Roman camp, up to the guards. Armed guard cohorts stood constantly by the Roman camp, relieving one another. And there is a harsh law concerning these cohorts: whoever leaves the guard post for any reason shall be executed. Therefore the guards chose a glorious death in battle instead of shameful execution and stood firm. And others, ashamed before those who had remained, returned to their aid. The Jews attacked without fear, overwhelming the Romans with their bodies.

And Titus came from the Tower of Antonia and sternly addressed his soldiers: “You have conquered the walls of foreigners, but why do you not think of your own? You have let the Jews out against you like prisoners from a dungeon.” And Titus gathered chosen horsemen and attacked the enemy from the flank. The Jews rushed at him and fought. Both armies clashed, and the dust prevented the eyes from seeing, while the general shout prevented the ears from hearing. No one could recognize either friend or foe.
