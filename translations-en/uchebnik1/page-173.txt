It is not now a time for lamentation, but for telling what happened. I shall describe also the remaining deeds of the rebels.

Simon Niger, when he fought with John, that is, Johanan, burned all the buildings with wheat, which would have sufficed for more than twenty years; by this he cut the sinews of the city and handed the city over to foreigners. The warring rebels tormented and tore the people like a huge body. The old men and women could not endure the internal calamities and prayed that an external army might attack them. By this alone they hoped to be freed from the evils that surrounded them. And those who suffered did not dare to weep over their sufferings, but shutting themselves in so that no one might hear, they groaned with horror.

The rebels walked over corpses as over haystacks and fought. John, that is, Johanan, made throwing engines from the wood prepared for the temple.

For the high priests had decided, together with the people, before this time to fortify the temple and raise its walls by twenty cubits. And King Agrippa brought from Lebanon the timber needed for this, beautiful in appearance and straight, in great quantity, and spent much gold and labor on it. And when the strife began, John, that is, Johanan, cut up this timber.

And John made throwing engines and towers in the number he needed for war. These lawless structures, as John hoped, would help him defeat internal enemies. But God made John's labor vain and brought Titus with the Romans before the battle between the rebels began.

Titus gathered his soldiers and sent for the distant legions, and went from Caesarea with three leading legions, with which Titus's father had ravaged Judea. With Titus was also the 12th legion, which, while Cestius was still commanding the army, had been defeated by the Jews; but this legion was renowned for its courage, and the soldiers remembered their former disgrace, and came eager, wishing to avenge it. Titus ordered the fifth legion to meet him at Emmaus, and the tenth at Jericho.

The neighboring kings also gave Titus soldiers as aid, who came in place of those whom Vespasian had sent to Italy. Tiberius Alexander also came to Titus. He had formerly been governor of Egypt, but now Titus made him chief of the commanders, since Tiberius had been the very first to honor and recognize Titus's father as emperor, and also surpassed all in age and experience of military service.

And Titus entered the land of the enemy. First went the soldiers sent by the kings, and all the forces of the allies; after them went the road-makers, and they drove the pack animals and carried the baggage of the commanders, and the soldiers rode.
