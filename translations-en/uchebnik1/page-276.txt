And they informed the king of all the accounts about Helena, and how the image and likeness of him born of her had been discovered in the Serpny place—an infant shining with a royal appearance. Hearing this, Consta was at first astonished at what the elders told him, and then, understandably, began to question them about Helena, wishing to see her. He also remembered the vision he had seen with Helena: a dark night illuminated by sunbeams.

Understanding that the vision had come true and learning of its fulfillment, he received the messengers worthily and, honoring them with gifts, sent them with many varied instructions to the Persian land, with a carriage and many wagons, with directions for many comforts and with gifts sent by the king, to bring Helena with the youth Constantine.

They, arriving quickly, took Helena with the youth and seated her in the royal carriage, and brought them to King Consta. When he saw them, Consta rejoiced greatly over both, embraced the youth and kissed him, loved the young woman also, and led them inside the royal palace.

Instructed by his father in the holy books, the all-wise Saint Constantine possessed all things; from his youth he had a royal mind and piety, and the wise virtue of manliness.

Rumor of the remarkable case with Helena spread, and this report reached Consta’s lawful wife, named Theodora, the true daughter of Herculius. By her he had three sons and a daughter, whose names were Constantius, Anvalian, Dalmatius, and the daughter Constantia, wife of Licinius. Of these, not one was worthy of the father’s kingdom.

Consta feared lest out of jealousy they might kill the young woman with her son by treachery, and with foresight he sent the youth with letters to King Diocletian and to his son-in-law Maximian, who were then living in Bithynia, in the city of Nicomedia. Having received the king’s son at the royal court, they assigned to him a detachment of imperial guards, since he was of royal offspring, that is, a pledge of peace, and he grew up in the palace.

Consta feared the same in regard to Helena also, lest his wife Theodora devise something against her, as he had already said before. When the youth Constantine grew up, he was taught the Hellenic sciences. But the demon who hates good stirred up envious men against him—idolaters from among the sorcerers and soothsayers living under Diocletian and Maximian Herculius. And foreseeing that he would become not only emperor but also a pious king and a destroyer and ruiner of the Hellenic heresy, and also a great preacher of God’s kingdom of Christ, for this reason they devised manifold and every kind of plot against him, to kill him.
