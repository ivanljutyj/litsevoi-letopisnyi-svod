To the end of their days the zealots were tormented with various torments, and an evil death overtook the wicked. But, to speak the truth, they were tormented not in accordance with their deeds, for they suffered less pain than that which they had inflicted.

The Roman commander Silva came to Masada with all his army and surrounded the fortress with a wall of siegeworks, so that no one could escape, and he set guards. Then Silva chose a place convenient for taking the city and entrenched himself there. The rock on which the fortress stood approached the mountain at this place. It was difficult for the Romans to bring what was needed to the camp. Food was carried from afar with great sweat, and water also, for the whole army, since there was no well anywhere nearby.

Silva took care of everything necessary and began to build an embankment, which required great skill and labor. The stronghold of the fortress had this nature: the rock on which Masada stood was round, large, and high; around the rock were deep ravines, so that even the bottom could not be seen. Two roads led into the city, on both sides of which were ravines. No livestock could by any means enter the fortress, only one man on foot could; and if someone came toward him, it was impossible even to pass by, but one had to stand motionless while the other squeezed past him sideways. And if someone slipped and fell into the ravine, even his bones could not be found.

At this place the high priest Jonathan originally built a fortress, then still deserted, and called it Masada.

After this King Herod saw Masada in such a state and zealously set about improving it. He raised a wall and enclosed with it the space on the summit of the rock, seven versts in circumference. The wall was of white stone, its height was twelve cubits, its thickness eight cubits. Herod also built thirty-seven towers, and houses, and palaces.

On that summit the soil was soft and fertile, and the king gave it to the townspeople to cultivate, so that in case of siege they would not experience a shortage of food; they cultivated this same land and gathered the harvest.

By his palace Herod set up four towers, each sixty cubits high. He also built baths and many covered passages. And he set up columns of white marble to adorn the palaces and for strengthening. In the houses and in the rocks in many places hollows were made for collecting rainwater, which flowed into a reservoir like a lake. There was a passage under the rock by which one could go unnoticed to its summit. Thus by human hands this fortress was strengthened.
