Vespasian, wishing to honor the king, decided to return the cities to him and sent his son Titus to Caesarea, so that Titus might take the army there and lead it to Tiberias.

And Vespasian encamped on Mount Sennabris, which lies thirty versts from Tiberias, and sent the centurion Valerian and fifty horsemen so that he might speak with the townspeople about peace, for Vespasian had learned that the people of Tiberias desired peace, but the rebels were stirring them to revolt.

Having ridden up to Tiberias, Valerian dismounted near the city himself, and so did those who were with him, so that the townspeople would not think he had come to fight. And before he could say a word, the rebels ran out of the city with weapons and rushed at the Romans. Among the rebels the chief leader was Jesus, son of Saphat. But Valerian could not violate his commander’s instruction and fight them. He feared the Roman law, by which whoever enters battle without orders is condemned to death, even if he wins. And Valerian was terrified by the sudden sortie of the Jews and fled on foot; his five soldiers also fled, leaving their horses.

Those who were with Jesus took the five horses as great booty and gain and led them into the city with songs.

The leading men of the city, seeing this lawlessness, ran to Vespasian and brought to him the king’s intercessor, so that he would not destroy the whole good people because of a small number of the wicked, but would spare the peaceful inhabitants and punish those stirring up rebellion. Commander Vespasian heard their request and, wishing to please Agrippa, the intercessor, promised to fulfill what they asked.

And learning of this, Jesus with his companions fled to Taricheae. Vespasian pursued him. Vespasian also sent his commander Trajan to find out whether the inhabitants of Tiberias were peacefully disposed.

Learning that the inhabitants of Tiberias desired peace, Vespasian went to them with all his army, and they opened the gates and met him with praise. And Vespasian ordered his commanders not to kill anyone.

Leaving Tiberias, Vespasian began to entrench between that city and Taricheae. He felt that Taricheae was warlike and would not soon make peace, since Jesus and all the rebels with him had fled there, hoping in the strength of the walls and in Lake Gennesaret, where there were many ships.

And the rebels saw the Romans entrenching, and they turned against them, advanced on them, and drove them off.

When the Romans began to array themselves against the rebels, they rushed to the ships and sailed away from the shore and began shooting at the Romans.
