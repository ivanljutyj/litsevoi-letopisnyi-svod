On the Idols of the Greeks

Among the Greeks people made idols, and wishing to instill fear in those who saw these idols, they said that the idol had been sent from heaven by Zeus and had flown down, and that it was better than any work made by human hand, and that it would not perish; therefore they called it “Diopetes,” that is, “fallen from heaven,” and “the heavenly image”; and they called it an image (in Greek, bretas), because it was known as a man (in Greek, brotos), though in truth this was not so. Such vain and deceptive fame about idols circulated, and the idol-makers were either killed, or they fled to other lands, so that no one would say that the idol was made by hands, but all believed that it was not made by hands; and the deceivers put this false rumor into people’s ears. It was by this lie that the city of Ephesus was deceived. That idol-makers were in truth killed is testified by what happened quite recently in Alexandria.

On the Idols of Egypt

When Ptolemy was in Egypt, he gathered craftsmen to make him an idol of Artemis. After the work was finished, having dug a great pit and hiding the deception, Ptolemy ordered the craftsmen to sup in it. When they were supping there, they were covered over and died, and received a fitting recompense for their work. Ptolemy did not want to reveal those craftsmen, because he did not want everyone to consider this supposed god to be made by hands, since he had declared it not made by hands. And so Ptolemy did. And behold, our Lord and God soon brought to light his most foul murder, and there was a great earthquake, and the shrine fell, and at the same time the pit was torn open, and the instruments of deception were revealed. And of all the cities of Egypt, the greatest idolatry was in the city of Canopus. And they say there was a certain wonder there.
