And Florus departed for Caesarea with the rest of the army, and sent a letter to Cestius, governor of Syria, in which he reported the disobedience of the Jews and falsely accused them of having supposedly instigated all that terrible slaughter from which they themselves had suffered.

The elders of Jerusalem and the high priests also did not remain silent, but, joining with Berenice, sister of King Agrippa, they wrote a letter to Cestius about the lawlessness of Florus.

Cestius, governor of Syria, received both letters—from Florus and from the high priests—and deliberated on them with his commanders. Some of them said that Cestius himself should go to Jerusalem with soldiers, either to execute the rebellious Jews or to investigate Florus's conduct, but that he should first send before him a sensible and loyal man to examine the matter of dispute and its meaning and to verify what the Jews were saying. And Cestius sent one of his chiliarchs, whose name was Neapolitanus.

At Jamnia Neapolitanus met King Agrippa, who was returning from Alexandria. The Jewish high priests and elders also came there to greet the king, to attend him, and to show him honor. Then with tears they began to tell of their misfortune and of the cruelty of Florus. Agrippa was angry with Florus, but prudently turned his anger upon the Jews; because he loved them, he wished to curb their insolence, so that they would not become proud from having suffered unjustly. And then the noble Jews who had suffered began to desire peace, realizing that the king was angry with them out of love for them.

The people went out from Jerusalem sixty versts to greet the king and the nobleman Neapolitanus, sent by Cestius, governor of Syria. And the wives of the slain wept, walking in front, and cried out and asked that Florus be punished.

And the Jews took Neapolitanus with one attendant and led him into the city, and told him of the desolation of the temple and the plundering, and said that they were obedient to all the Romans and hostile only to Florus.

Neapolitanus, seeing the meekness and humility of the Jews, entered the temple and praised them greatly, since they were loyal and obedient to the Romans, and paid honor to the sanctuary as far as was possible for a pagan.
