There was a wall with a rampart thirty-nine versts long, and behind it they built thirteen fortresses. All this was built in thirteen days.

And the Romans besieged the whole city with fury. Titus stationed guards around the city. During the first night watch he himself kept guard; during the second, Tiberius; during the third, the commanders. All night the guards went around the rampart in detachments.

The Jews’ hope of salvation was cut off. Famine spread through houses and families and crushed everyone. Houses filled with dead women and children, while old men lay in the streets—some still breathing, others dying. Some of those still breathing entered their graves alive. The living could not bury the dead. Some went to bury others and died over the dead. Youths and maidens, swollen from hunger, wandered in circles through the city, lifeless as idols, as phantoms. There was no one to weep or wail, for people’s throats had dried up from hunger. Everything revealed their weakness: their eyes had dimmed and their mouths stank. Those still breathing counted the dead happy and envied their rest. A deep silence enveloped the city, gloomy and full of death. The robbers looked at the dying and laughed; they tested spears and swords on them and stripped clothes from the dead. And each one, as he gave up the ghost and saw the robbers alive, groaned and looked toward the temple. Those same men, unable to endure the stench of the corpses, at first ordered them buried and paid for burial from the temple treasury. Later they began throwing them from the walls into the ravines.

During a circuit, Titus saw ravines full of corpses and pus flowing from bodies; he wept, groaned, called God to witness, and said: “I did not do this; you have done it to yourselves.” And none of the rebels made a sortie, since they too were gripped by hunger and fatigue, so the Romans remained in prosperity and cheer. They had wheat in abundance, and all necessities were brought from Syria and other nearby regions. Many Romans stood near the walls and showed food to the besieged, taunting them and inflaming their hunger by their own satiety. But the rebels remained unyielding even in these sufferings.

Titus sought to preserve the remaining population of the city and began to build siege ramparts in four places near the Tower of Antonia. It was hard for the soldiers to carry wood and earth from 120 versts away, for all the surroundings of the city had been cut down. The emperor rode around the troops and urged on their work. By this he showed the robbers that they were already in his hands.
