When Titus had killed the guilty, he ordered the local inhabitants to be spared and the slaughter stopped. The rebels who had earlier fled to the lake saw that the city was taken and sailed farther off.

Titus sent a horseman to his father with the joyful news of the victory at Taricheae. When Vespasian learned of it, he rejoiced greatly at his son’s fortitude and valor, for the greater part of the enemy had been defeated. And Vespasian ordered that the city be surrounded and that all who fled from it be cut down.

And going out to the lake, Vespasian ordered rafts to be built and to sail after the ships. They quickly built them, having plenty of wood and workers, and overtook those fleeing in the ships and killed them. Altogether the rebels killed at Taricheae numbered 16 thousand.

On the Lake of Gennesaret and the Jordan

That lake is called Gennesar after the adjoining land. In width it is 40 versts and in length 100. Its water is pleasant to drink, light, and clear. Beautiful sandy shores surround the lake. If the night is clear, the water becomes cold as snow. The fish in the lake are of various kinds and differ from the fish of other places both in appearance and in taste.

Through this lake the Jordan flows, and many think that there the Jordan is born. But this is not so. There it only comes to the surface; it flows hidden under the ground, and is born from another lake called Phiala. This lake is situated, if one goes toward Trachonitis, 120 versts from Caesarea, to the right of the road. It is called Phiala because it is round. Its water is constant: it neither decreases nor increases nor overflows. It was not known whence the Jordan begins. But Philip, ruler of Trachonitis, made this clear. He poured chaff into Phiala and afterward found it floating out at the place where, as people in old times thought, the Jordan takes its beginning. The Jordan, flowing out of that lake, runs through Lake Samachonitis among marshes and forests, then after 120 versts passes the city of Julias and flows through the Lake of Gennesaret. And afterward, passing many deserts, it empties into the Asphalt Lake. Around the Lake of Gennesaret there is land which is also called by that name. The nature there is wondrous and beautiful. All kinds of fruit trees grow on it because of the fertility of the land. And the local inhabitants planted it all, and everywhere laid out great gardens. The air there is favorable for the most varied plants. Nut trees, which love cold, are born there in countless numbers. Date palms, which live in warmth, and fig trees and olive trees grow in abundance, like a forest.
