The Jews stabbed Romans in battle; some, who had only climbed up and had not yet managed to raise a shield, the Jews got ahead of and cut down. Other Jews pushed the ladders away from the walls and threw them down.

In some places the Romans raised the imperial standards. The Jews rushed forward and made a fierce slaughter, trying to seize the standards. The Romans considered the loss of a standard a great disgrace and did not retreat, but fought until all had perished.

The Jews seized the standards and killed those who had climbed onto the walls, and boasted as if they had killed or taken captive the emperor himself. Yet not one of the Romans died without revenge, but first killed, and then was killed.

Titus, seeing that his mercy toward the Temple was harming the soldiers, ordered the gates to be set on fire. Then Ananus from Emmaus and Archelaus, son of Magadatus, defected to him, hoping to receive pardon from Titus, since they defected at the time when the Jews were prevailing. Titus thought they had come with deceit, for he knew of the Jews’ inflexibility, and was already about to kill them, and said: “You came under compulsion, not of your own free will, and are unworthy of salvation, since you defected when you saw your fatherland ablaze.” But because of his given word Titus restrained his anger and forgave them, though he did not grant them terms equal to those of the other defectors.

The soldiers set fire to the silver gates, and when the silver melted and ran, the flame engulfed the boards, and from there unexpectedly spread along the passageways. The Jews saw the flame around them, and their bodies and souls weakened, and out of terror no one even tried to extinguish the fire, but they stood in one place and watched.

Then, greatly grieved, the Jews came to their senses, but did not come to reason; instead they grew even more angry at the Romans. All that day and all night the Romans set fire to houses and covered passageways.

On the next day Titus sent men to extinguish the fire and to level with the ground what had been burned, and to widen the path for the army. And Titus summoned six of the chief commanders: Tiberius Alexander, Sextus Cerealis, Larcius Lepidus, Titus Frigius, Fronto Eternius, and Marcus Antonius Julianus, governor of Judea; and Titus consulted with them about the Temple.
