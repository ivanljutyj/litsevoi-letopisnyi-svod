Many Thracian cities Thomas besieged by sea and by land, conquered and ravaged, enslaving their inhabitants and bringing with him many misfortunes.

He reached even the reigning city itself, but since Almighty God did not aid him, his intentions had unstable foundations, and so they soon collapsed; he only defiled his hands with murders. And although Constantinople did not have Hezekiah, a pious and virtuous king, yet the Lover of Mankind and Helper of all, the Lord, not for the sake of the deluded ruler perishing from great folly, but having regard to the will and faith of His faithful ones in distress, says: “For the sake of the wretched and the poor, for the sighing of the needy... now I will arise and protect the city for this, but for My sake and for the sake of the Seed that sprang from David, My son, and for the sake of the new Jerusalem, which I have chosen.” For under the impious Ahab the king of Syria, having gathered countless troops against him, went to war against him, saying to Ahab: “If you open Samaria with your palms, all the people and my footmen...” But soon God, the Giver of all good things, easily crushed the king of Syria, warning the thrice-wretched Ahab beforehand: “Behold, I give him into your hands, so that you may know that I am the Lord.” And it was as said. And so soon all knew the power of God, and the God-inspired psalmist sang: “The Lord is strong and mighty, the Lord is mighty in battle” — in the battle that took place the Israelites struck down 120,000 enemies. God’s punishment overtook them: when those left alive fled into a certain city, the city wall collapsed and buried 27,000 people beneath it.

Thus Thomas, with the pride proper to barbarians and with Assyrian cunning, approached Constantinople, imagining that he would easily subdue God’s city, that is, the city of Saint Constantine. But although the siege, lasting a whole year, was permitted by God because of our sins, he could achieve nothing of what he hoped, but was defeated. Because of his pride he lost even what he had, and he himself soon perished. For not only did the inhabitants of Constantinople send a numerous army against him, but help was also given them, and they fought with such courage and inspiration that they soon won victory, putting to shame the enemy’s arrogance.

For Thomas’s strength, on which he relied, exalting itself in every way in his folly, cast him himself into the very pit that he was preparing for his enemies. His numerous ships were burned, his best soldiers and commanders were killed and taken captive, and his army was scattered. Surrounded on all sides by enemies, he remained in confusion and despair.

Also all the cities he had subdued, invincible and indestructible as it seemed to him, were burned to the ground and laid waste. And so “the house of David, the city of Christ, advanced and grew strong, while the house of Saul, a remnant of remaining toil, advanced and grew faint,” and God was truly glorified.
