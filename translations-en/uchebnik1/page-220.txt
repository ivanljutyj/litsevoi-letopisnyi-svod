The Jews stabbed the Romans in battle; and some who had only climbed up and had not yet managed to raise their shields, the Jews got ahead of and cut down. Other Jews pushed the ladders away from the walls and threw them down.

The Romans in some places raised the imperial standards. The Jews rushed at them and made a fierce slaughter, trying to seize the standards. The Romans considered the loss of a standard a great disgrace and did not retreat, but fought until all had perished.

The Jews seized the standards and killed those who had climbed onto the walls, and boasted as if they had killed or taken captive the emperor himself. But not one of the Romans died unavenged; each first killed and only then was killed.

Titus, seeing that his mercy toward the temple was harming the soldiers, ordered the gates to be set on fire. At that time Ananus of Emmaus and Archelaus, son of Magadat, deserted to him in the hope of receiving pardon from Titus, since they had deserted when the Jews were prevailing. Titus thought they had come with deceit, for he knew the Jews’ inflexibility, and he was already minded to kill them, and said: “You have come under compulsion, not of your own good will, and are unworthy of salvation, since you deserted when you saw your fatherland ablaze.” But for the sake of his word Titus restrained his anger and pardoned them; yet he did not grant them terms equal to those in which the other deserters remained.

The soldiers set fire to the silver gates, and when the silver melted and ran, the flame seized the wooden boards and from there unexpectedly spread along the passages. When the Jews saw the flames around them, their bodies and souls grew weak, and from terror no one even tried to extinguish the fire, but they stood in one place and watched.

Then, greatly grieved, the Jews came to their senses, but did not come to reason; instead they became even more enraged at the Romans. All that day and all night the Romans kept setting fire to houses and covered passages.

On the next day Titus sent to extinguish the fire and to level with the ground what had been burned, and to widen the path for the army. And Titus summoned six of the chief commanders: Tiberius Alexander, and Sextus Cerealis, and Larcius Lepidus, and Titus Phrygius, and Fronto Eternius, and Marcus Antonius Julianus, governor of Judea; and Titus took counsel with them about the temple.
