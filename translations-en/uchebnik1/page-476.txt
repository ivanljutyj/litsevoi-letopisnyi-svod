Learn why Paul said that there is “a revelation which it is not lawful for man to utter.” If the word is impossible, yet God’s power is wholly ineffable. Paradise is perceptible, yet not temporal, and has both a present and an eternal perception. It is not spoken of Paradise in a parable; it was not planted as a figure, since it appears eternal. After five thousand Paul saw it; he reached there. Paradise is perceptible, yet not temporal, and the promise that is in it is on trees, but not temporal. “And further, the metropolis Jerusalem, now temporarily decaying, is chosen as dwellings for the incorruptible nature and eternal God; and by God’s will a little afterward, God, incorruptible and luminous and again in being, will be a righteous dwelling, according to Gromov—that is, the preacher of the Son.”

In the Revelation of John, Jerusalem is God-created, God-guarded, God-adorned, and God-beloved. David says: “For the Lord loved the gates of Zion more than all the dwellings of Jacob.” David magnifies Jerusalem as the glorious city of God, saying: “Glorious things are spoken of you, O city of God,” and again: “As we have heard, so in the city of the Lord of hosts, in the city of our God: God has founded it forever.” This holy and desired mysterious land, glorious and praiseworthy, from which the first man came, from which all prophets and apostles sprouted—that is, were born—and moreover the Mother of God, higher than all existing heavenly and earthly creatures, sprouted—that is, was born—and gave birth to the Lord and Creator of all and Master, and was partaker of salvation; the wicked and utterly impious Saracens seized this Holy Land, the divine and glorious Mount Zion, and the holy metropolis—that is, the capital—of the great King and our Savior, Jerusalem. When they had seized that Holy Land, they greatly multiplied and grew strong.

And the Saracen prince named Muawiya prepared many ships, and he sailed to the village called Phoenice, which is in Lycia, where Constans was with Greek soldiers.

The king was preparing to fight in the ships in the morning, and the night before he saw himself in a dream in Thessalonica, that is, Salonika. The king quickly told this to a certain wise man, and he said to him: “O king, it is a bad dream, for your stay in Thessalonica prefigures that victory is appointed to another; this means that you are to be defeated, and victory will be given to the enemies.”

The day came, and the soldiers drew up in battle order, and King Constans was defeated. The sea mingled with Greek blood.
