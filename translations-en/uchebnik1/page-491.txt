Whatever evil was devised by others, Constantine began to cultivate by the thousands. The bride, adorned with gold and made youthful, the Holy Church, to whom Christ, the pure Bridegroom, was betrothed, Constantine suddenly seized from the very bridal chambers—alas!—and showed her as an aged widow and mourning. The fruitful vine, the cluster with good seed, this most fierce one, like a long-toothed goat leaping from somewhere out of the meadow, tore out by the root. This demonic one shattered her beauty and tore the sacred images from the Church. In those places he depicted with colors—that is, paints—and with psephoi—that is, mosaic pebbles—his beloved horse racing and images of lions and other beasts, and called “heavenly” that which is not even worthy of earth.

The Saracens, however, remained in civil strife and dissension. Hearing of this, King Constantine set out with his soldiers for the Syrian lands.

For the sake of such order and tranquility, King Constantine by decree also took his kinsmen, Armenians and Syrians, heretics, and settled them near Byzantium; most of them settled in Thrace; and these are now the so-called God-sufferers, or Theopaschites, followers of the Monophysite doctrine of the accursed Peter the Fuller.

On the Signs and on the Plague. At that time unexpected and unprecedented signs began: certain crosses appeared in great numbers on people’s clothing and on the holy church vestments. And then, by the wrath of God, there came a boil-like ailment, a crimson, putrid disease, a deadly sickness, and not only those in the city but also in the surrounding cities rotted away.

There were also certain visions for many people, and terrors for some: it seemed to them that certain strange, terrible, and strong persons were walking about, and they met them as their friends, and called them by some name, and conversed with them. This was in winter. In spring the disease spread still more, and at harvest—that is, in summer—it flared up so greatly that all the houses were completely shut up, so that because of the great calamity they laid the dead on pack animals and on carts and carried them out.

When the tombs in the cities and villages were filled, it was ordered to dig also in the valleys and in the orchards—that is, gardens—inside the old city, for human burial. Along with all this, wrath fell upon the unconsecrated patriarch: the accursed disease called chordapsos, or inflammation of the intestines, ended his life.

The tormentor king, however, left the city of Constantine and went to Nicomedia, and remained there a long time.
