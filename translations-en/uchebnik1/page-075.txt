"I shall provide these my grandchildren with good protectors. I give the daughter of Pheroras in marriage to the elder son of Alexander, and let Pheroras be his father-in-law and a mighty guardian. And let the son of Antipater take as wife the daughter of Aristobulus, and my son Herod—her sister. And if we arrange all thus, their fathers will be, as it were, immortal. And whoever loves me, let him reason likewise, and let no one oppose my will. I pray God that He bless these unions and make them useful to my kingdom and my grandchildren, and that He look mercifully upon these children." And having said this, Herod wept and ordered those whom he had joined in marriage unions to give one another their hands. Then Antipater grew dark, and all clearly saw that he was greatly grieved. For he considered the honor shown to the orphans ruinous to him, and again he began to fear everything, since the sons of Alexander had as protector Archelaus, king of Cappadocia, and also Pheroras the tetrarch. Antipater also felt the people's hatred toward him, for all the Jews pitied the brothers.

Therefore Antipater tried to dissolve these marriage unions. However, he feared his father, cruel and clever. And Antipater came to Herod and asked him not to deprive him of the honor with which he had honored him, and Antipater said: "Why did you let me be called king, yet give the power to others? How can I reign when the son of Alexander, whose grandfather is Archelaus and whose father-in-law will be Pheroras, grows up and comes to manhood? But if you wish, strengthen my rights to the kingdom and separate the unions ruinous to me, and let them marry others." The king had nine wives: Doris bore Antipater; Mariamne, daughter of the high priest, bore Herod; Malthace of Samaria bore two sons, Antipas and Archelaus, and a daughter Olympias; Cleopatra of Jerusalem bore Herod and Philip; Pallas bore Phasael; Phaedra bore Roxana; Elpis bore Salome; two wives were childless.

Antipater asked Herod to change the marriage unions. And the king was greatly angered, seeing Antipater's cruelty and merciless attitude toward the orphans. And Herod thought of the sons he had killed, that they too had probably become victims of slander and evil intent. And then with fury Herod rejected Antipater's request and drove him from his sight.

But soon Herod took Antipater back and yielded to his flattery, and listened to his persuasions. Anyone can understand what power there was in Antipater's flattering speeches from the fact that even Salome, Herod's sister, many times asked him for herself, but each time received refusal.

Salome asked—and the empress supported her in this request—that Herod give her in marriage to the Arabian commander Syllaeus. But Herod did not listen to her and, moreover, swore that she would be his worst enemy if she did not abandon this intention.
