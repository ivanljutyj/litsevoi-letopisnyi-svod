He gave them over to fire, showing this especially toward the Orthodox. What more is there to say?

From his demonic madness and impiety, his servants and soldiers cut off the noses of many righteous people. As once the head of the Forerunner was cut off and brought to Herod, so to the Herod among us they brought what had been cut off, as a sweet and pleasing gift, laid upon a platter. The gouging out of eyes, the burning of beards, and the shedding of blood are witnessed even to this day by those who bear the marks of these torments on themselves. But his violent and wondrous death only the depths of the sea and the stones know. His victories were the edifices of his darkened, demonic accomplices.

Also at the end of his evil, God-opposing life, being consumed by swift fire and flame, he foresaw in reality, as in a mirror, the flame of Gehenna awaiting him and the gnawing of the unceasing worms, and Copronymus cried out mightily without ceasing: “Gehenna of rest and likeness,” which means “O Gehenna, abode worthy.” Resting in such and so great virtues—such as those in which Diocletian and Maximian, as we know, ended their lives—the accursed one was thus painfully released and brought down, ending his foul and evil life, a denier and apostate of Christ, enemy of all His saints, and vile rebel.

The Thirty-Fifth Reign: Leo, who reigned in Constantine’s City

After Constantine, called Copronymus, reigned Leo, his son by a Khazar woman, and he reigned 15 years.

This same emperor chose to be pious for a short time. After this, however, he was deceived. He dared to place on his head a crown from the Great Church, and his head was terribly burned.

And so, possessed by a fierce fire, he died, having by deceit deprived himself of sacred dignity.

How a hound, that is, a dog, killed the murderer of its master. In the reign of that same emperor, a certain man was walking alone, and a hound, that is, a dog, followed him. A robber was walking along the same road, and killed that man and fled, while the hound remained in that place and guarded the body of its master.

A certain man was walking along that road, and saw the body, and was moved with compassion, and dug the earth, and buried it. The hound, that is, the dog, saw that the man had done a good deed, was moved, and followed the burier, kindly doing him service. That man was an innkeeper; and the hound would escort all who came and went from the innkeeper’s house.
