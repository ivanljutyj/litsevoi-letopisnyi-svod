Born of fear, as well as the habit of enduring hardships. The Jews also had hope of salvation. The Romans hoped to take Jerusalem. Hardships did not stop either side. They struck the walls and fought at the walls every day. Sorties were made, weapons were broken, and gnashing and cries rose to the clouds. Some groaned and wept, others raised victory cries. War in all its manifestations was there. And in the morning they began fighting almost as soon as they had parted for the night.

At night both sides did not sleep, and night was worse than day. The Jews feared that the walls would soon be attacked, while the Romans feared a sudden sortie against them. Both sides spent the night under arms, and at dawn they were ready for battle. The Jews vied with one another as to who would be the first killed before the eyes of their commander. Such respect and obedience did they have for their rulers in those last days that if they had been ordered to cut their own throats, they would have done so. The Romans strove valiantly, for they were accustomed to conquer and had experience of ceaseless warfare, and they were also conscious of the greatness of their empire, which Titus led. The emperor rode everywhere, and all the soldiers saw him, and each of them sought to avoid shame and was ashamed to appear weak in the emperor’s eyes. And the one who had suffered greatly in battle rejoiced, since all had seen his sufferings, and the emperor might honor him. Therefore many displayed courage beyond their strength.

One day the Jews drew up in battle array before the walls and shot from a distance. A certain Longinus, a horseman of the Roman army, rode into the Jewish detachment and broke their formation, and with one spear thrust killed two of the strongest fighters, and galloped back to his own. Many imitated such valor. The Jews prowled like wild beasts, despising death, and looked only to kill one of the enemy, and they accepted death easily if they also killed an opponent.

Titus did not allow the soldiers to fight recklessly, and said: “A mad attack is akin to despair. True courage is to carry out every killing prudently, and to stab enemies without harm to oneself and without losses.” And Titus brought the “ram” to the middle tower on the north side. There a certain Jew, a sorcerer named Castor, gathered around himself ten men like himself, and waited for Titus, and stretched out his hands, and began piteously to call him, as though begging him for mercy and that Titus would stop striking the wall and not break the tower. Titus believed him in his simplicity and thought that all the Jews were repenting, and told his men not to strike the tower with the “ram,” and ordered the archers not to shoot at those who were begging.
