He sent against them two regiments of Roman soldiers who were guarding the city, and five thousand Libyan soldiers then there, for the destruction of the Jews. And Alexander ordered not only to kill the Jews but also to plunder their property and set fire to their houses.

And the soldiers rushed to the place called Delta, where the Jews lived, and carried out what they had been ordered, but not without losses, for the Jews returned with weapons and resisted for a long time. However, the first successful breakthrough of the Roman soldiers brought death to the Jews. The Romans did not spare Jewish infants, and they were not ashamed to kill old men either, and fifty thousand were killed that day.

And none of them would have remained if the surviving Jews had not sent to Alexander. He pardoned them and ordered the soldiers to stop the slaughter, and the soldiers, accustomed to obey quickly, carried out the command.

But the Alexandrian people, full of hatred toward the Jews, did not spare even their dead bodies. Such an end then befell the Jews living in Alexandria. But Cestius, governor of Syria, saw that everywhere they had begun to fight, and considered that it was time to act.

Cestius took from Antioch the twelfth legion entire, and from the other legions two thousand each, and six cohorts of infantry, and four squadrons of cavalry, and from King Antiochus two thousand cavalry and three thousand archers, and from Agrippa, king of Commagene, as many, and from Sohemus, ruler of Emesa, and from the nearby cities a multitude.

Cestius headed for the Galilean city called Zabulon, also called Andron, and found that there were no people in it, for all had fled into the forests. The city was full of property and every kind of wealth. And Cestius gave the city to the soldiers for plunder, and then burned the city. Its temples were beautiful, like those in Tyre and Sidon. After this Cestius ravaged the land of Zabulon.

And Cestius burned the villages around Zabulon and returned to Ptolemais. ... The Romans set fire to the tower in which one of the Jewish leaders, Niger of Perea, had hidden. In the tower he found an underground chamber and, entering it, saved himself from the fire. When after three days the Jews came, he went out to his own, and they were filled with unexpected joy, since God had preserved the leader and commander for the future war.
