This Julian took the monastic tonsure and went to Athens. He greatly desired to reign and sought in Hellas a sorcerer who would foretell that he would receive the reign he so desired. And behold, an impious man, a sorcerer, came to Julian and said: “Your desire will be fulfilled.”

The sorcerer took Julian, led him into an idolatrous shrine, into a dark place, and summoned deceitful demons. Julian was frightened by the multitude of apparitions and placed the sign of the Cross on his forehead. The demons saw this and vanished. The magician understood what Julian had done, was angered at him, and said: “The demons did not flee because they feared the sign of the Cross, as you think, but they fled when they saw your abomination and falsehood.” And so the magician finally seduced the wretched Julian, and washed away the divine baptism from him with the blood of idolatrous sacrifices, and thus Julian lost his piety.

Constantius again feared to rule such vast domains alone, and he considered that relatives would be more reliable than strangers, and summoned Julian, the brother of Gallus, and to strengthen the union with him gave him his sister Helen in marriage, and Constantius appointed Julian Caesar.

With Julian, Constantius went to war against the Persians. When Constantius returned from Persia, he appointed Julian autocrat. Constantius himself died in Cilicia.

Under this Emperor Constantius a sign appeared in the sky at noon—the image of the Precious Cross shone brighter than the sun from Golgotha to the Mount of Olives; this was under Cyril the Patriarch.

Under Constantius, in the 25th year of his reign, Basil the Great became metropolitan in Caesarea; he was then 37 years old, and he held the episcopal throne for 8 years. And Gregory the Theologian was bishop in Nazianzus. Under Constantius, Antony’s disciples also labored: the Great Hilarion and Macarius of Egypt; under him also labored Macarius of Alexandria, Pambo, and Theodore the Sanctified; under this same autocrat Ephrem the Syrian, very renowned, also labored.

This Ephrem the Syrian left many writings, containing thirty thousand verses, which the Syrians sing, and because of this sweet singing the so-called Bardesians, heretics, were delivered from their delusion. Under Constantius, Flavian of Antioch was the first of all to order singing in church in two choirs, and this custom spread to all the ends of the universe.
