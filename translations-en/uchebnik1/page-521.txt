On when the canons appeared. Venerable Andrew of Crete lived in the reign of Constantine the Bearded. During the Sixth Ecumenical Council he was an archdeacon, and later became Archbishop of Crete. He was the first composer of canons and wrote the Great Canon and other canons for feasts and saints. He was followed by Venerable John of Damascus, who lived in the time of Leo the Isaurian and his son Constantine Copronymus. He wrote the Hirmologion and the Theotokarion of the Mother of God, full of profit and compunction, and many canons for feasts and saints. Together with him, his companion Bishop Cosmas of Maiuma also composed many canons. In the reigns of Nicephorus and Leo the Armenian, after the Seventh Ecumenical Council, Theodore the Studite and his brother Joseph, Bishop of Thessalonica, wrote the Triodion. In the reign of Michael and Theodora, Theophanes the Branded, Bishop of Nicaea, and Joseph the Hymnographer, a long time after the Seventh Ecumenical Council, wrote the Octoechos in eight tones and canons for many feasts and saints. Their successors continued thereafter to compose canons for newly glorified saints, supplementing the church services with them.

On the rejection of meat for monks. The ascetic fathers did not eat meat from the beginning. Some say that the prohibition for monks to eat meat was established at the Fifth Ecumenical Council, but there is no evidence of this in any of the conciliar definitions. Theodore the Studite lived after the Seventh Ecumenical Council, seven hundred years less twenty before the end of the seventh millennium, and seven hundred years before our time. And he writes in his Rule for the brethren: “For the Dormition of the Mother of God, slaughter two oxen for the brethren.” If the eating of meat by monks had been forbidden at any council, he would not have commanded this or written it in the Rule. Nikon, however, in the last part of his Epistle writes that “now, by the grace of God, meat is completely forbidden to monks,” while mentioning no council, but simply saying “now.” And Nikon lived four hundred twenty years before the end of the seventh millennium, and even longer before our time.
