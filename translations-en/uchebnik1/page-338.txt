And then Julian gave his word to these two Persians that he would not kill them if they led the Roman soldiers out of the desert. At the second hour of the same day Julian walked among the soldiers and exhorted them to end the tumult, and was suddenly wounded, and entered his tent, and died that same night, as Marcellinus wrote.

And they found Julian lying face down. Suddenly a spear was hurled at him through the prayers of Basil the Great of Caesarea. The martyr Mercury killed Julian, because he had spoken wicked words about the Most Pure Mother of God. And Julian’s muscle was pierced, and the spear entered his ribs, and he did not know who had killed him. And no one knows to this day whether a man or an angel did it, having become a servant of God’s will.

This same Julian dared to open pagan shrines; and foul services began to be performed in them, while God’s churches were razed to their foundations and Christians cruelly tortured. In the cities of Ascalon and Gaza there were holy men and women who had preserved chastity during their lives; and their bellies were cut open, barley was poured in, and they were given to the swine to be eaten; and the unclean consumed their entrails and blood. In the city of Sebaste they opened the ark of the holy Forerunner and consigned it to fire, and scattered the relics and ashes of the saint.

Julian made war on the Christians: inside and around the cities he defiled water springs, sprinkling them with the blood of idolatrous sacrifices. He also defiled what was sold in the marketplace, sprinkling bread, meat, vegetables, grain, and other foodstuffs with the blood of sacrifices to idols. Christians saw this and groaned and wept. Under Julian were martyred Porphyry the actor, and Juventinus, and Maximus, and Poplia, a church servant from Antioch, and Artemius the Wonderworker, and Cyriacus, who revealed the Precious Cross to Empress Helena, as well as Elpidius, Eustochius, Marcellus, Gemellus of Paphlagonia, Matrona from the land of Paphlagonia, Agathia from the city of Panormus, Eustathius, bishop of Bithynia, Mark, bishop of Arethusa, Cyril the deacon, and with him Timothy, bishop of the city of Prusa in Bithynia, as well as Patermuthius, Coprius, and Alexander from Egypt, Aemilian from the city of Dorostolum, John the Soldier, and Eugenius of Antioch.

Emperor Julian, having seated himself on the imperial throne, introduced this change into imperial custom: he filled a pagan shrine with coals and placed incense on the altar before him, and commanded everyone who wished to take gold from him first to throw incense onto the sacrificial coals, and only then to take the gold from his hands. And the impious one commanded that his image on the coin be inscribed: “Zeus” and “Ares.”
