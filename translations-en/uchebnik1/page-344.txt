About Moses

There was a certain woman named Moavia, a Saracen queen, who was a Christian and came from a Greek family. She had been taken captive, and because of her beauty the Saracen king loved her. She spent many years in rule and caused much harm to the Greeks, so that she destroyed Greek soldiers with her great warriors and captured many cities in Palestine and Arabia.

The Greeks begged her to make peace. She answered, “You will have peace only when you appoint as bishop the monk named Moses in my country.” That monk lived in the desert with Christians and shone with virtues and miracles.

The queen’s request was conveyed to the Greek king. The king ordered his commanders to fulfill this request as quickly as possible. And Moses was brought to Alexandria so that Luke might appoint him bishop.

Moses refused and said, “Luke shall not lay his hands on me, for they have been stained with the blood of the saints.” Luke was put to shame, and, heeding the people’s need, had to fall silent and allow Moses to receive ordination from the bishops whom he had expelled, as indeed happened.

And when Moses had been appointed bishop, he was given to Queen Moavia, and he made many of the Saracen people Christians. Much is told about this people—where it takes its origin and whence it received its name, and that they are circumcised at thirteen years. To Edessa, the city situated between the rivers, where the apostle Thomas taught, Valens came and saw a multitude of orthodox people, expelled from the church and performing the service in the field. And Valens was so inflamed with anger that with his own hands he struck the governor in the face, because the Christian people had not been expelled from there, as the king had commanded them.

The Greeks and the king kept pressing the governor and urging him to execute the Christians. But the governor, out of love for humanity, made this known to the townspeople before the day on which he intended to go out to destroy the people, for he secretly sent them word to be on their guard and not to go to the cathedral service.

When the next day the governor came with a great commander, then, contrary to his custom, he tried to frighten the people and did everything so as not to destroy a single one of them. Yet he saw that even more people had gathered in that place, and the people kept arriving. And many tried to come earlier, as if they feared that someone would not be put to death.
