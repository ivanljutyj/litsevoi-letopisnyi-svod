Archelaus sent his commander and asked the rebels to cease their disturbances.

When this commander came into the temple, he could not say a word, for he was stoned. The people were ready to fight. Then came the Feast of Unleavened Bread, also called Passover, when many sacrifices are offered to God, and an innumerable multitude of people came from all the land for the service. The instigators of the revolt stood secretly in the temple, and suddenly they leapt up, and all were thrown into confusion.

Archelaus sent soldiers to seize the chief instigators. Then all the people rushed at the soldiers and killed many of those sent with clubs, and the commander barely escaped, covered in blood. After this, as though they had done nothing wrong, the people went to offer sacrifice.

Archelaus, seeing that the people would not calm down without bloodshed, sent his soldiers against them: foot soldiers through the city and horsemen through the field; the soldiers attacked those offering sacrifice and killed a thousand of them. “Their feast is turned into mourning,” as it is said. The rest of the people fled to the mountains and forests. Then Archelaus’s messengers appeared and announced that all the people should disperse to their own lands and cease celebrating.

Archelaus took his mother and his nobles with him and set out for Rome. Antipas followed after him, disputing Archelaus’s right to reign and saying that the first testament should be believed, not the last. Salome and all the relatives supported Antipas. But everyone hated Archelaus.

Herod’s relatives wished either to rule independently and be subject directly to Roman authority, or to have Antipas as their king.

Antipas’s supporters wrote an accusation against Archelaus and delivered the letter to Caesar. Archelaus also delivered a message to Caesar, attaching his father’s testament, the ring, and the crown. Caesar examined both reports and considered the size of the kingdom, the amount of tribute collected, and the number of Herod’s relatives. Caesar assembled his rulers and admitted Tiberius to this assembly, though he had not been admitted before up to this occasion. Caesar gave the parties time to set forth their rights to the throne. Then Salome’s son Antipater rose, an eloquent man, defender of Antipas and opponent and accuser of Archelaus, and said: “You, Archelaus, ask Caesar for permission to reign.
