He who looks upon the circle of time, for, as has already been said, fate had kept both the same month and the same day on which the Babylonians had earlier burned this temple. From the time of the first building of the temple, begun by King Solomon, to the present destruction, which occurred in the second year of Vespasian’s reign, there are reckoned 1130 years, 7 months, and 15 days. From the later building of the temple, carried out by Haggai in the second year of Cyrus, to this conquest there are reckoned 639 years, one month, and 15 days.

When the temple was burning, the Romans seized and plundered everything they found, and everyone they overtook they put to death with iron; there was no mercy for any age, nor respect for comeliness, but children, old men, the vile, and priests alike they delivered to death. War displayed every means of killing and of pleading for mercy. The flame crackled, and the cries of the dying and their groans made a vast noise. Because of the height of the hill and the size of the burning building, one who looked from afar could have said that the whole city was burning. And it is impossible to imagine anything more savage and terrible than the outcry that then arose in Jerusalem. For the Roman troops shouted with excitement. The rebels screamed, surrounded by fire and weapons. The people, as many as remained, fled, crying out in horror, and wept before the approaching torment. Many, withered by hunger, seeing the temple burning, again found the strength to shout and weep. And the surrounding hills and forests echoed in answer to that cry. Everywhere there was turmoil and terrible suffering. And it seemed that the temple mount was being torn from its foundation, since it was wholly engulfed in flame. And there were more slain than slayers. And the earth could not be seen, for it was all covered with corpses.

The soldiers, stepping over heaps of the dead, overtook the fugitives. The rebels barely fought their way through and barely withdrew to the outer temple, and from there to the city. The remaining people hid themselves in the outer passages.

The priests first hurled at the Romans the iron spits that they had in abundance; then, when the fire reached them, they retreated to a wall eight cubits wide. Two of the noble men rushed into the fire and burned with the temple: Meir, son of Bilgi, and Joseph, son of Dalai. The Romans considered it superfluous to spare the buildings near the temple if the temple had already been burned.

And the Romans set fire to the surviving passages, the doors, and the gates, except two: the eastern and the southern. But later they changed their minds and destroyed those gates also to the foundation. The Romans also burned the treasury, in which much wealth lay: garments, fabrics, and countless other things. And it is in no way possible to recount all the Jewish wealth that had been gathered in the treasury from all the rich families.
