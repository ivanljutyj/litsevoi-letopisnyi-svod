Born of fear, and also the habit of enduring hardships. The Jews also had hope of salvation. The Romans hoped to take Jerusalem. Difficulties did not stop either side. They struck the walls and fought at the walls every day. Sorties were made, weapons were broken, and gnashing and wails rose to the clouds. Some groaned and wept, others raised victorious cries. War was there in all its manifestations. And in the morning they began to fight, having barely parted for the night.

At night both sides did not sleep, and night was worse than day. The Jews feared that the walls would soon be attacked, and the Romans feared that a sortie would be made against them suddenly. Both sides spent the night under arms, and at dawn were ready for battle. The Jews competed with one another as to who would be the first killed before the eyes of their commander. Such respect and obedience did they have for their rulers in those last days that if they had been ordered to cut their own throats, they would have done so. The Romans strove in courage, for they were accustomed to victory and had experience of incessant warfare, and they also understood the greatness of their empire, which Titus led. The emperor rode everywhere, and all the soldiers saw him, and each of them sought to avoid shame, and was ashamed to appear weak in the emperor's eyes. And the one who had suffered greatly in battle rejoiced, since all had seen his sufferings, and the emperor could honor him. Therefore many displayed courage beyond their strength.

Once the Jews drew up in battle array before the walls and shot from a distance. A certain Longinus, a horseman of the Roman army, rode into the Jewish detachment and broke their ranks, and killed two of the strongest fighters with one spear thrust, and galloped back to his own. Many imitated such valor. The Jews prowled like wild beasts, despising death, and looked only to kill one of the enemies, and accepted death easily if they also killed an opponent.

Titus did not allow his soldiers to fight recklessly, and said: "A mad assault is akin to despair. True courage is to commit every killing with prudence, and to stab enemies without harm to oneself or losses." And Titus brought the "ram" to the middle tower on the north side. There a certain Jew, a sorcerer named Castor, gathered around himself ten men like himself and waited for Titus, and stretched out his hands and began piteously to call to him, as though begging him for mercy and that Titus would stop striking the wall and not break down the towers. Titus believed him in his simplicity and thought that all the Jews were repenting, and told his men not to strike the tower with the "ram," and ordered the archers not to shoot at those pleading.
