In Italy Vespasian’s relatives will help us: his brother and his second son; the former’s soldiers will obey him, and to the latter the sons of nobles will join. If we delay even further, the Roman counselors themselves will send and appoint as emperor the one whom we, standing nearby, disdain.” So spoke Vespasian’s commanders, and with universal approval they proclaimed him emperor and asked him to accept rule and save those who had fallen into trouble. Vespasian had already been grieved by what was happening in his fatherland, but he did not wish to rule himself. Although by his deeds he considered himself worthy of power, he preferred the secure life of a subordinate to the anxious life of a ruler. Vespasian refused decisively, but the commanders pressed and urged him. The soldiers surrounded Vespasian with drawn swords and threatened to kill him if he did not accept the title of emperor. Vespasian argued with them for a long time and did not want to take this burden upon himself. But since his soldiers obeyed him, this time he obeyed them too. And first Vespasian decided to secure Alexandria for himself, for he knew that Egypt was the most important part of the empire, since grain was brought from there to Rome; and if he took Alexandria and halted the export of grain to Rome, Vitellius’s rule would inevitably collapse, since the Romans would not want to endure hunger: either they would drive Vitellius out or kill him. Vespasian also knew that Egypt was a secure land, and if some unforeseen disaster occurred, he would be safe there, for one cannot pass along the coast—the desert is waterless—and by sea there is no harbor, while by the rivers there are terrible rapids.

Therefore Vespasian wished to obtain Egypt in order to strengthen his power. And then he sent to Tiberius, who governed Egypt and Alexandria, and informed him of the valor of the army, and that he himself had of necessity been yoked into the yoke of imperial rule, and that Vespasian would like Tiberius to be his helper.

Tiberius read the letter and rejoiced greatly, and commanded the army and the people to swear loyalty to Vespasian and to prepare everything needed for his arrival.

And faster than thought the eastern sovereigns were informed of Vespasian’s proclamation as emperor, and all the cities joyfully received this good news, celebrated, and offered sacrifices for Vespasian. The Roman troops in Moesia and Pannonia, who until then had been for Vitellius, received Vespasian with great joy.
