And he promised the supplicant Apsimar to sell the maimed Justinian for gold. O gold, most insolent traitor! O gold, seller of friends and murderer of kin! How you accomplish even what cannot be done, and force nature and kinship to be despised. But what must come to pass by God’s judgments, by the Providence of God, neither an iron wall nor fiery force will hold back. For Justinian learned of the khagan’s deceit and conspiracy, and fled from there, and attached to himself—that is, favorably disposed—Tervel, the Bulgarian prince, by many promises.

Justinian came to the Byzantine land, to the reigning city itself, and brought many legions of Tervel’s army. With a few of them Justinian took counsel and gained their trust, and with them found the water-bearing conduit, that is, the aqueduct, in order to cut off the water conducted into the city, for Justinian knew this from former times. And because of thirst for water the city was delivered to Justinian.

The Twenty-ninth Reign, of Justinian the Slit-Nosed, that is, with the cut-off nose, who reigned in the city of Constantine

After Apsimar, Justinian the Slit-Nosed received the kingdom a second time, and remained so for seven years, in which he did many evils, which this narrative will set forth. To the Bulgarian prince Justinian, fulfilling what was promised, gave most abundant gifts.

In the city of Constantine Justinian gave over to fire many of the boyar families, and plundered their estates and herds.

Of great men Justinian slaughtered many; he killed Leontius together with Apsimar, as robbers and thieves, that is, plunderers, by his heavy scepter. Thus the wicked are subject to judgment: even if at first they escape torment, afterward they receive worse torment. Leontius and Apsimar received a twofold, that is, double, vengeance: not only did they themselves suffer, but they were also guilty of the misfortune of many others. Justinian’s avenging sword struck with torments not only common people but also boyars, because he himself had suffered at their hands.

Callinicus, who was patriarch in Constantinople, Justinian tortured and mocked. And he wrought outrage even upon children, the unreasoning and small. Finally Justinian extinguished for Patriarch Callinicus the bodily lights of the heart—his eyes—and sent him into exile in Rome.

And in place of Callinicus Justinian appointed as patriarch Cyrus the Recluse, who had been in the Amastris monastery, since he had foretold to him: “You will reign again.”

Justinian became exceedingly angry with all, and as if he had gone mad, and killed countless people from the townsfolk and from the army; to some he openly cut off heads, and others at night, sewn into sacks, he threw into the sea.
