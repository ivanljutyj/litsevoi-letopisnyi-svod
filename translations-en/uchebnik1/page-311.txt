On the Victory of Constantine over Licinius

Licinius, however, who had hypocritically aided the blessed Emperor Constantine out of cunning, cut off from Christianity the Roman lands in the vicinity of the most blessed city of Nicomedia and the city of Heraclea. And serving idols, he began to blaspheme Christ and to cut off the heads of Christians.

The third week came after two weeks of festal rest because of the Resurrection: the first dedicated to the Passion and Crucifixion of the Lord, the second to the Resurrection.

Yet the holy Emperor Constantine the Great, having enjoyed so many of God’s benefits and having established the faith, did not cease every day to offer grateful praise to the Giver of blessings.

Deep peace came to Rome, and great calm for the faithful, who rejoiced and were glad because there was no threat. And gradually all peoples came to the Christian faith and were baptized, and they shattered their fathers’ idols.

This Licinius the Emperor Constantine had appointed Caesar in Nicomedia. But Licinius, having a Hellenic upbringing, hated Christians and, not daring openly to resolve upon persecuting them for fear of Emperor Constantine, secretly nursed his malice. When he had separated himself and was ruling in his own regions, he began persecutions, and first he expelled the God-loving Christians who were in the senate, and without remorse he consigned to oblivion the oaths previously given to holy Great Constantine, never ceasing to kill Christians, so that many martyrs of Christ appeared in various places. He killed forty monks from Adrian, a Macedonian city, and Ammon the deacon, their teacher; also killed were Seurian from the city of Sebaste, Macrobius and Gordian from the Paphlagonian country, Zoticus and Lucian, Zeno and Valerian, and Julian the presbyter with forty-nine with him, Nicetas, Atticus, Eudoxius, Agapius and those with him in the city of Sebaste, Abibus the deacon, Gordius from Caesarea in Cappadocia, Hermolaus the deacon, Stratonicus, Theodotus, bishop of Cyrene in Cyprus, Theodore Stratelates, Blaise, bishop of Sebaste, the Forty Martyrs in Sebaste, Basil, bishop of Amasea, Irenia, daughter of Emperor Licinius, Dorotheus, bishop of Tyre, forty-five martyrs in Nicopolis of Armenia, Acacius, Adrian, son of Emperor Probus.

The blessed Emperor Constantine sent letters to him, commanding the disobedient one to cease his rebellion. Then the pious Constantine learned that Licinius had stirred up fierce persecutions and was slandering his benefactor.

And Constantine armed himself against him by land and by sea, and soon troops gathered for battle in Bithynia. And Licinius was defeated at Chalcedon.
