After taking counsel, the Jews decided first to try fighting the Romans; they made bold sorties every day and fought the enemy, and many Jews perished, but they also killed many Romans. Victory went to the side that seized the moment: the Jews won if they managed to attack the Romans unexpectedly, while they were not defending themselves; the Romans won if they noticed the sortie in time; then they had time to arm themselves and beat the Jews. But it was not these skirmishes that led to the capture of the city, for another opportunity arose for the Romans to force the Jews to surrender.

There was among the Jews a young man of Machaerus named Eleazar, very brave and strong. He became famous in the sorties, for he urged his countrymen to fight the Romans, and wounded many Romans, and did them great harm, going out before all and returning after all.

And one day the Jews and Romans separated after the battle, and some withdrew to the city, others to the siege ramp.

On the Courage of Rufus

Eleazar stood before the city gates, reviling the enemy and boasting before them, for he did not expect anyone to attack him. And Eleazar addressed his people standing on the walls and thought only of them. And suddenly one of the Roman soldiers named Rufus, an Egyptian by birth, seizing a convenient moment when no one expected it, rushed suddenly at Eleazar and seized him and lifted him onto his shoulders together with his weapons. All on the walls were terrified, and Rufus quickly carried Eleazar off to the Romans.

The Roman commander ordered the young man to be stripped, stretched out in view of the townspeople, and beaten. And great pity for him seized the Jews, and the whole city cried out and wept, as though not one man but all were being tortured.

When Bassus saw how the young man's torments affected the Jews, he decided to use this to conquer the city and prepared to bring their pity to such a point that they would be forced to surrender the city and surrender themselves. And Bassus ordered a cross to be set up for the crucifixion of Eleazar. Seeing this, the townspeople grieved still more and cried out unbearably. Then Eleazar too began to entreat them not to abandon him and not to give him over to a cursed and shameful death, but to think of saving him and also preserving their own lives, and to submit to Roman power and fortune.

And when the young man spoke thus, his family inside the fortress joined his entreaties. He was of noble birth. And then the Jews yielded. And they sent envoys quickly, and began negotiations, and promised to surrender the city if Eleazar were not killed. And the Roman commander listened to them.
