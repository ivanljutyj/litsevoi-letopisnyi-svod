Titus was grieved that he could not soon avenge John’s deceit, but his wrath was assuaged by the multitude of captives and slain.

And Titus entered the city of Gischala and ordered one corner of the wall to be razed to its foundations as a sign that the city had been taken. The rebels who remained among the local population Titus subdued by threats, not by executions. For he knew that if he began to execute the guilty, people would accuse even the innocent out of personal enmity and hatred.

And Titus left guards in Gischala, so that the rebels would fear and the peaceful inhabitants would feel more secure. Thus was completed the conquest of Galilee, which cost the Romans much blood and sweat.

When John came to Jerusalem, the whole city came out to him and crowded around him in thousands and tens of thousands, asking him about the calamities brought by the invading enemy. John and those with him were gasping from their swift flight, and by this they showed their plight and misfortune; yet even in misfortune they bore themselves proudly and said: “We have not come to save ourselves from the Romans, but wishing to fight them from the stronghold of Jerusalem. For it is foolish and useless to spend strength fighting for Gischala and other small cities. It is fitting that we preserve all our strength for the defense of the capital.” From the words of John and those with him many understood that the city of Gischala had been taken, and that their arrival was not a withdrawal but a flight. And all the people were inexpressibly grieved, seeing in this a foretoken that they too would be conquered.

John was not ashamed of his lie, and he went up to everyone, rousing them to war, and bade them be strong; the Roman army he counted as nothing, but the Jewish forces he praised. With such speeches John deceived the people, exploiting their ignorance and simplicity, and he said to them: “Even if the Romans grow wings, they will not be able to fly over the walls of Jerusalem. The Romans have already exhausted their army and broken their siege engines in taking the Galilean cities, small and weak.” The people listened to these false speeches, and many young men joined John and rose for war. But sensible and elderly people, foreseeing what would happen, wept as though they had already been conquered together with the city.

Titus, however, went from Gischala to Caesarea.

Vespasian, meanwhile, rose from Caesarea and went against Jamnia and Azotus, and the inhabitants of these cities surrendered to him.

Taking men from Jamnia and Azotus, Vespasian returned to Caesarea. And in all the cities of Judea there was great turmoil and strife. When the Jews had freed themselves from Roman rule and breathed freely, they turned against one another, and those who thirsted for war fought against those who wanted peace.
