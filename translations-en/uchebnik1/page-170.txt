Vitellius’s men resisted Antonius briefly, then fled to Cremona, a city on the border of Gaul and Italy. Antonius blocked all their retreat routes and cut down most of them before the city, and gave the city over to his soldiers for plunder. Many visiting merchants and local inhabitants of Cremona were plundered. And all Vitellius’s soldiers, 30,200 men, were killed. And of Antonius’s soldiers, 4,500 men were killed.

Antonius released Caecina and sent him to Vespasian to inform him of what had happened. Caecina came to Vespasian and was received, and Vespasian covered the shame of his treachery with great honor. Since Antonius was near Rome,

Sabinus, Vespasian’s brother, who was in Rome, felt confident, gathered the night guards, and entered with them the Capitol, the highest place in the city. By day many of the leading men of Rome joined Sabinus. Also there was his nephew Domitian, the son of Vespasian, on whose influence those who had opposed Vitellius in Rome relied.

Antonius troubled Vitellius little, but he was enraged against those who had opposed him in Rome with Sabinus. In his usual cruelty, Vitellius desired the blood of noble Romans and sent against the Capitol all the troops he had.

And there was a great battle in the Capitol between the supporters of Vespasian, led by Sabinus and Domitian, and Vitellius with the soldiers he had brought from Germany. The latter were three times more numerous than the former, and Vitellius won. Domitian and other leading men fled, and the soldiers defending them were all cut down.

Sabinus was brought to Vitellius and killed. And Vitellius’s soldiers plundered the Capitol and burned the temple of Jupiter.

A day later Antonius rode up with his soldiers, and Vitellius’s troops met him and came out of the city, and in three places joined battle with the enemy, and all Vitellius’s troops were routed.

Vitellius came out of the palace drunk. He despaired and drank and ate as if for the last time. They dragged him to the middle of the city, inflicted many wounds on him, and cut him down. Vitellius was emperor for 8 months and 5 days. Had his life continued, the whole empire would not have sufficed for his intemperance and drunkenness. And 150,000 of Vitellius’s soldiers were killed on the third day of the month Apellaeus, that is, December.
