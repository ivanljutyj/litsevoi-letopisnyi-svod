The whole land is inhabited by Galileans, and there is no unoccupied place in it; everywhere there are frequent cities, and villages as numerous as the stars, and the people cannot be counted, so that in the smallest village live sixteen thousand people. The gardens yield mainly oil, wine, and dates. Cold streams flowing down from the mountains irrigate them. ...

Now let us tell of the Romans. Their vast kingdom was not given to them by some miracle; they themselves obtained it by wisdom and their own sweat. For not only in wartime do they take up arms and begin to act. As if born with weapons, they never part with them, and do not wait for a special time, but every day, as if at war, they exercise.

Therefore battle is easy for the Romans; fear does not seize them, fatigue does not overcome them, and the disorder of battle does not break their battle order. Confident victory over their enemies accompanies them everywhere. If anyone calls their mock drills bloodless battles, he will not depart from the truth.

When they come into enemy land, they do not begin to fight until they have dug in. The place where they wish to halt they level, if it is uneven.

And not everyone makes the camp, nor is it made haphazardly, but the builders take their tools and measure out a four-cornered space, within which they place the tents, while they surround the outer circuit as if with a wall, and along this circuit they place all the engines of war.

They make gates at each of the four corners, such as to allow entry and exit for pack animals, and broad enough.

They make streets in the camp between the tents, and in the middle they set up the commander’s tent like a temple. In the Roman camp a separate place is also allotted to merchants, so that they may trade in all necessities; artisans too are placed separately, and so are the leaders of the army.

There is also a place near the commander with special seats for judges. There disputes are tried. If the judges cannot decide a dispute, the disputants are led to the commanders of the detachments.

If the commanders of the detachments too have difficulty deciding a dispute, they turn to the commander of the army. The Romans set up walls and dig in faster than thought, thanks to the number and skill of the workers. They dig a ditch around the camp four cubits deep and four wide. Life in the camp goes quietly and calmly, and nothing is done among them without reason and without orders.

Whether they go for fodder, or for wood, or for water, they always ask permission and secure a guard for themselves. Roman soldiers do not eat or drink on their own authority, but they have trumpets that produce various sounds, by which they know when to do what.
