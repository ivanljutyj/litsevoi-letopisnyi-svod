This Julian had his hair tonsured in the monastic manner and went to Athens. He greatly desired to reign and in Hellas sought a sorcerer who would foretell that he would obtain the kingdom he so desired. And behold, an impious man, a sorcerer, came to Julian and said: “Your desire will be fulfilled.”

And the sorcerer took Julian, led him into an idolatrous temple, to a dark place, and summoned the deceiving demons. Julian was frightened by the multitude of phantoms and placed the sign of the cross on his forehead. The demons saw this and vanished. The magician understood what Julian had done, grew angry with him, and said: “The demons did not flee because they were afraid of the sign of the cross, as you think, but they fled when they saw your vileness and falsehood.” And so the magician finally seduced the wretched Julian, and washed away the divine baptism from him with the blood of idolatrous sacrifices, and thus Julian lost his piety.

Constantius again feared to rule such vast domains alone, and he considered relatives more reliable than strangers, and summoned Julian, the brother of Gallus, and to strengthen the alliance with him gave his sister Helena to him in marriage, and Constantius appointed Julian Caesar.

And Constantius went with Julian to war against the Persians. When Constantius returned from Persia, he appointed Julian autocrat. Constantius himself died in Cilicia.

Under this Emperor Constantius a sign appeared in the sky at noon—the image of the Precious Cross shone brighter than the sun from Golgotha to the Mount of Olives; this was under Patriarch Cyril.

Under Constantius, in the 25th year of his reign, Basil the Great became metropolitan of Caesarea; he was then 37 years old and occupied the episcopal throne for 8 years. And Gregory the Theologian was bishop in Nazianzus. Under Constantius there also labored the disciples of Anthony: Hilarion the Great and Macarius of Egypt; under him also labored Macarius of Alexandria, Pambo, and Theodore the Sanctified; under this same autocrat there also labored Ephrem the Syrian, very renowned.

This Ephrem the Syrian left many writings, containing thirty thousand verses, which the Syrians sing, and because of this sweet singing the so-called Vardisians, heretics, were delivered from their delusion. Under Constantius, Flavian of Antioch was the first to order singing in church in two choirs, and this custom spread to all the ends of the universe.
