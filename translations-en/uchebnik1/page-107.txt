The city belongs to them, since it was built in honor of Caesar. The Syrians said: “We do not deny that the city was built by Herod. But he gave it to us, the Greeks, and therefore adorned it with images and a temple with idols. From this alone it is clear that the city was not created for the Jews, for Jews may not have any image in their cities.” In the course of the quarrel the inhabitants began to take up arms. The authorities were concerned to stop the killings, punished and shackled the rebels. Felix killed and plundered many disobedient Jews, and sent some to Nero to determine to whom the city belonged.

And the Caesarean Greeks came with a letter from the emperor, in which he declared that the city belonged to the Greeks, not to the Jews. This laid the beginning of the war, in the twelfth year of Nero’s reign, in the seventeenth year of Agrippa’s rule. The strife began from something small, but it brought so much evil that it is impossible to express. The Jews of Caesarea had a synagogue in the city, and the owner of the place where the synagogue stood was a Greek. Many times the Jews wanted to buy that place, and offered far more for it than it was actually worth. But the owner would not yield and harmed the Jews; he enclosed that place and set up workshops on it, so that the Jews were left a very narrow and inconvenient path to their synagogue. At first young men came and hindered the building on the place. Then John the tax collector went with the leading men among the Jews to the governor Florus and gave him eight talents of silver, so that he would forbid the Greek to build on the place. But the governor took the money and left the city.

On the next day was the Sabbath, and the Jews gathered at the synagogue. And a Greek set up an altar where the Jews entered, and began to slaughter birds on it and offer sacrifice, by which he irreparably offended the Jews, since he trampled their law and defiled the place. The wise and calm among the Jews said that they should go to the governor, but the foolish young men called for war. The Caesarean Greeks armed themselves. And Jucundus, an elder of the cavalry, came and removed the Greek altar from the entrance to the synagogue, and wished to reconcile those who were at enmity. But the Greeks did not listen.

Then the Jews took the sacred books and fled to Narbata, a city sixty versts from Caesarea.

And John and twelve other men among the Jews came to the governor Florus and complained with tears, and told him what had happened, and begged him submissively, and mentioned the eight talents which they had given him. But he seized them and shackled them, accusing them of having carried the sacred books out of Caesarea. The governor also sent to the temple and took from there seventeen talents, supposedly in order to spend them.
