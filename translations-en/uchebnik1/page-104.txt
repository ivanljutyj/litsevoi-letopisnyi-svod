And many servants of the Wonderworker about whom we wrote earlier appeared under these rulers. And people spoke of their Teacher, that He is alive although He died, and that He would free the Jews from slavery. And a multitude of people listened to these speeches and heeded the commands of the servants. And they were heeded not because of their nobility, for they were from simple people: some tailors, others cobblers, others craftsmen, but they were heeded because they performed wondrous signs, truly doing everything they wished. The nobles and rulers, seeing movement among the people, decided with the scribes to seize the servants and kill them. Then they were ashamed and afraid of the signs, and said: “Such miracles are not done by sorcery. If they are not from God, they will soon be exposed.” And the servants remained free. Later some of them were detained and sent away, some to the emperor, others to Antioch, and others to distant lands to investigate these matters.

After those two Roman rulers, Claudius sent Cumanus to Judea. Under him there was weeping and death for the Jews. The people came to Jerusalem for the so-called Feast of Unleavened Bread, and Roman detachments stationed themselves on the roof of the temple. Such was their custom, to keep order with arms during the festival, so that the Jews would not make a disturbance relying on their multitude. One soldier lifted his garment from behind, turned his back to the Jews, shamelessly thrust out his buttocks, and broke wind at them with the corresponding sound, toward where the sacrifice was being offered. Then all the people were enraged and shouted to Cumanus to punish the soldier quickly. Some foolish youths wanted to fight; they seized stones and threw them at the soldiers. Cumanus feared that the whole people would rush upon him, and summoned still more soldiers. As they began to arrive, unbearable fear fell upon the Jews, and they fled from the temple through the city in all directions. Such was the crowding and confusion among them that they trampled one another. And more than 1,000 people fell. And instead of a festival there was wailing and weeping in all houses. To this calamity another was added.

Robbers on the road at Beth-horon, near Jerusalem, attacked a certain Stephen. Cumanus sent to the villages near that place to put their inhabitants in chains, because they had not pursued the robbers or caught them. And there one soldier found books of the Holy Law, trampled on them, and threw them into the fire. The Jews considered that their whole land had been burned. Bound as if by a rope by piety, they all rushed with one desire: either to die or to kill that soldier.
