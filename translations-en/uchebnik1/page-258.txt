They will remember, and therefore we justly accept vengeance. I tell you and entreat you to accept vengeance not from our enemies, the Romans, but from our Lord, against whom we have sinned; for His torments are lighter. And let our wives die undefiled, and let our children not know slavery. After them we too will die nobly, and do good to one another, and make a glorious burial of our freedom. But first let us consign our fortress and our property to the fire. Indeed I know that the Romans will be grieved if they gain neither our bodies nor any spoil. Only let us leave the food, so that it may testify that we died not under compulsion and not from hunger, but by our own free decision—to accept death instead of slavery.” So spoke Eleazar, and not all liked his speech. Some hastened to obey him and were filled with joy, considering death a good. But those of the sicarii who were weaker pitied their wives and families and their own premature end, and looked at one another in tears and wept, and by this showed that they did not desire death.

Eleazar, seeing how frightened and crushed they were by the greatness of the deed he was urging them to do, himself feared lest by their tears they soften even the firm. And Eleazar continued his speech: “In place of Jerusalem there is only a camp of conquerors; of the former people only old men sit on the ashes, and women walk about, left by the Romans to be violated. Who, thinking of this, can still see the sun? Who is so hostile to his fatherland? Who loves his own soul so much and is so heartless that he wishes to live longer in this world? It would be well for us all to die sooner, rather than see the holy city laid waste by the hands of enemies. Before, we still had hope of escaping the enemy, but that hope has proved vain. And now we are left as the last, without any hope. Let us strive to die with valor. Let us show mercy to ourselves, to our children, and to our wives, while it is still possible. We were born to death, and those born of us were born to death. Death cannot be escaped even by kings. But to endure reproach and slavery, and to see one’s wives led with their children to be violated—this is not given to men according to the law of nature. But the cowardly, the foolish, and those who fear to die and not see this—let them accept such suffering. We, however, have been glorified for courage and have rejected the Roman yoke, and today, when the Romans called us to peace, we did not listen to them. And who does not know the fury of the Romans if they take us alive? Woe then to the young, for because of their youth and bodily strength they will be able to suffer great torments. Woe to the old, for they have no strength to endure the coming torments. Woe to the women, for they will suffer terrible violence from the conquerors. Woe to the children, for they will be dragged away bound—”
