For he made a feast for the demons by night and slaughtered a child. And I bear witness to this word: Suflai’s youth, whom the demonic one secretly slaughtered in reality, having made this a sacrifice and demonic honor to many gods.

Whatever evil others might invent, Constantine began to cultivate it by the thousands. The bride, adorned with gold and renewed in youth, the Holy Church, to whom Christ, the pure Bridegroom, was betrothed—Constantine suddenly seized her from the very bridal chambers—alas!—and showed her as an aged widow and lamenting. The fruitful vine, the cluster of good seed, this most savage one, like a long-toothed goat leaping up from somewhere out of the meadow, tore out by the root. This demonic one shattered the beauty and tore the sacred images from the Church. In those places he depicted with paints—that is, colors—and with mosaic pebbles—that is, tesserae—the horse racing dear to him and images of lions and other beasts, and he called “heavenly” that which is not even worthy of the earth.

The Saracens abided in internal strife and dissension. Hearing of this, Emperor Constantine went with his soldiers into the Syrian lands.

Because of such a situation and tranquility, Emperor Constantine by decree took his own kinsmen, Armenians and Syrians, heretics, and settled them near Byzantium; most of them settled in Thrace; and now these are the so-called God-sufferers, or Theopaschites, following the Monophysite teaching of the accursed Peter the Fuller.

On the Signs and the Pestilence

At that time unexpected and unseen signs began—crosses in great numbers appeared on people’s clothing and on the holy church vestments. And then by the wrath of God came an ulcerous ailment, a red dysentery, a deadly disease, and not only those in the city but also in the surrounding cities wasted away.

Also there were certain visions to many people, and to a certain part there were terrors: it seemed to them that certain strange, terrible, and strong faces were processing, and they met them as their friends, and somehow called them by name, and conversed with them. This was winter. In spring the disease spread still more, and at harvest, that is in summer, it flared up so greatly that all households shut themselves up completely, so that because of the great calamity they stacked the dead on cattle and carts and carried them out.

When the graves that were in the cities and villages were filled, then also in the valleys and in the orchards, that is gardens, within the old city, it was ordered to dig for human burial. With all this, wrath came upon the unholy patriarch—the accursed disease called chordapsos, or inflammation of the intestines, ended his life.

The tormentor emperor left the city of Constantine and went to Nicomedia, and remained there a long time.
