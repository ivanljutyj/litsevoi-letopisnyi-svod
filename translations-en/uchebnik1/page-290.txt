The aforementioned Consta, very tall and lean, called the Green because of the pallor of his face, was sent for this purpose by the emperors Diocletian and Maximian from Rome to the Persians. This Consta was the grandson of Emperor Claudius, who reigned before Aurelian and Diocletian. He took as his wife in Rome Theodora, daughter of Maximian Herculius and sister of Maxentius, who was killed in Rome; about him I will tell you later after this.

This same Consta, father of Saint Constantine, was a man good in character and strong of body. He was magnanimous as no other and invincible in battles, for which he was granted by the emperors the rank of tiro. He set out from old Rome together with three other renowned warriors; and they, having fought gloriously in various ranks, went to the Persians for a treaty.

As he passed with the men through Roman lands and cities, it happened that those warriors and the great Consta, weary from the march, came to the city of Serp in Dacia, which was later called Elenin-grad. Having arrived there, they found a convenient place at an inn. And the warriors wished to pitch their tent in the place they had chosen, in which, weary from the journey, they fell asleep, remaining there.

But Consta was inflamed with desire for intercourse with a woman, and in order to quell his uncontrollable passion he told the innkeeper of his wish. The innkeeper, seeing the man’s nobility, rejoiced and, taking his daughter Helena, who had not yet known men, gave her to him to satisfy his carnal passion. He, seeing her good conduct, exceptional beauty, and kindness, was enchanted by her.

And then Consta took the maiden as a concubine, and lived with her, and gave her a purple robe—a royal garment—for her bed when they were together. And all the nights he possessed her, and once the night was illuminated as day, and an unprecedented, most glorious vision appeared, for the sun unnaturally returned from its bed in the ocean, emitting rays out of season and illuminating that house on four sides where Consta was with the maiden. Consta marveled at the vision, a God-fearing and zealous defender of his faith, thinking that it was from Apollo, his protector from youth, and he remained sleepless, for he was afraid that he had not promised the maiden to be joined with her in marriage. And he did not guess that this unnatural vision was not anger, as it seemed to him because of his transgression, but by the image of daylight foretold changes for this place. And throughout the night he felt great anxiety.

When the sun rose, he summoned the girl’s father, possessed by great fear, and having calmed him with gifts, handed the girl over to him, begging him to keep her faithful to him as a pure and beautiful vessel, and if in the course of time a child should be born, he asked him to guard it likewise as the apple of his eye, supposing his own speedy death because of the vision.
