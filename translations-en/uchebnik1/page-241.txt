Antiochus, having received soldiers from the Roman ruler, fell upon his own kinsmen with cruelty and demanded of them that they should not observe the seventh day, that is the Sabbath, nor the seventh week, but should do the same as on other ordinary days. And so zealously did the Romans compel the Jews to transgress the Jewish law that not only in Antioch did they begin to violate and trample upon all that belonged to the seventh, but also in all the cities where Jews lived. Such were the calamities that the Jews of Antioch experienced at that time.

Afterward they again fell into other misfortunes. By chance the quadrangular council building burned, along with the palaces and the archive with the royal documents. And the same Antiochus who had come before came to the Antiochenes, laid this blame on the Jews, and set the Antiochenes against the Jews. The Antiochenes, adding this intolerable fury to their former anger, and as if the Jews had set them on fire, like madmen, as though driven on by shouts, rushed at the slandered. And Collega, the NEW ruler, barely managed to stop their onslaught; he told them that they must write to the emperor about what had happened.

Vespasian sent the former governor of Antioch, Caesennius Paetus, to another place. Collega investigated this matter carefully and got to the truth. As it turned out, none of the Jews slandered by Antiochus was involved in this crime. This deed was on the conscience of certain men, treacherous and lying, who thought that they would free themselves from their debts if they burned the council building and the records and books in which their debts were written, together with the palaces in which these records lay.

They reported to Titus that his father had reached Rome, that his arrival had been received with love in all the cities of Italy, and that Rome had received Vespasian with great enthusiasm and joy. Hearing all this, Titus gave himself over to great joy and gladness, for his worries about his father’s journey had come to an end.

While Vespasian was still far from Rome and Italy, the people of Italy received him with all their soul and all their mind, and hoped in him, and awaited his coming. Even before Vespasian came, they saw him before them in their mind’s eye as if he had come, and they received him. And such an attitude toward Vespasian was free from all compulsion and deceit. For all remembered how much evil they had suffered from the change of recent rulers, and all had one desire—soon to receive an emperor like Vespasian: venerable in age and in military deeds, and adorned with victories. And all understood that his rule would be for the benefit and salvation of his subjects.
