Then Vespasian set about taking the city. He ordered the strongest horsemen to dismount and go to the breached place in the wall, and after them he ordered the bravest foot soldiers to go. He placed the remaining horsemen along the slope of the mountain, so that none of the townspeople might escape. And he stationed archers and stone-throwers behind, so that they too would not let the townspeople come out from behind the city walls.

At other places where the walls were intact, Vespasian brought ladders, in order to draw part of the townspeople away from the breached place to defend those parts of the wall, and thereby enter the city more safely. Josephus saw this and ordered molten tin to be poured on those climbing the ladders, so that, burned by the tin, they fell. And many Romans were burned.

Then Vespasian ordered three towers to be made on the embankment, fifty cubits high, and bound them with iron, so that fire would not seize them and they would be steadier under weight.

He placed on these towers archers, javelin throwers, and light stone-throwing engines, and struck the besieged as if from heaven. The townspeople below could not fight the Romans above them, for the Romans were hidden from them in the towers, while the Jews were in plain sight. Thus the visible Jews yielded before the invisible Romans, but resisted to complete exhaustion.

Then there was a deserter from the city who urged Vespasian to attack, and he said: “The townspeople are already exhausted from constant wakefulness and fatigue. It will be easier for you to take the city in the morning hours, coming secretly. Sleep will overcome the besieged, who have fought day and night, at dawn. It is at this hour that they hope to rest from the struggle. All the guards will fall asleep too.” Vespasian did not fully trust the deserter, for he knew the steadfastness of the Jews and their loyalty to one another.

Before this deserter, Vespasian had once seized a resident of Jotapata and tortured him with all kinds of torments, both fire and iron, so that he would tell what was happening inside the city. But he said nothing and, crucified, died with a smile. However, the deserter’s words seemed to Vespasian not very far from the truth, and he put him under guard.

Vespasian himself, at the time the deserter indicated, came with soldiers to the city, keeping silence. And first Titus with Sabinus, commander of a thousand, climbed onto the walls; after them Sextus and Placidus with their soldiers, and they killed the guards and entered the city quietly.

And when the Romans walked through the city, the conquered did not hear it, for their strength was broken by weariness, and sleep felled them. If anyone did rise, he did not see the Romans because of the fog that descended then.
