There was another sign. Michael, who walked behind Leo, stepped on the hem of his royal garment, and Leo himself took this as a bad omen. The wondrous recluse Kiknos, adorned with every virtue, had originally said—and this was mentioned above—that both of them would reign.

Soon Leo appointed Michael patrician, and Thomas (mentioned third in the prophecy) tourmarch (commander of a tourma). At that time the Bulgars were ravaging Thrace. Leo, wishing to conclude peace with the Bulgars, sent a message to their prince. But the Bulgar prince did not want peace, having become proud over his victory over Michael Rhangabe.

Then Leo went to war against the Bulgars. But the Greeks who came did not exert themselves greatly in battle, and the Bulgars began to defeat them, and the Greeks began to retreat. Then Leo left the place where he had camped and concealed himself with his army in ambush on one of the hills. The Bulgars decided that King Leo had fled; this inspired them and deprived them of vigilance.

Leo, standing on the hilltop, saw the indecision of the Bulgars, called to his soldiers in a loud voice, and strengthened them. And the Greeks rushed upon the Bulgars, who were not expecting an attack, and defeated them, and killed many, and took others captive. And they nearly captured their prince Krum, but the Bulgars rushed to him in great numbers and gave him the opportunity to save himself. King Leo returned victoriously to the reigning city, bringing many captives with him.

After a short time he set himself against the veneration of holy icons and again kindled the hearth of the iconoclast heresy, which had been extinguished. It happened thus. The prophecy of the aforementioned Kiknos was fulfilled, for he had foretold that Leo the Armenian would reign. Remembering this, King Leo wished to thank and reward the elder and sent him many gifts with one of his intimates. But the one who came with the gifts found another monk, an iconoclast, settled in the cell of that holy elder (who by that time had departed to the Lord). The envoy, not knowing this, began to hand the gifts to the iconoclast monk. He not only did not accept the royal gifts but sent the envoy back with contempt, saying: “I will not accept gifts from a king who bows to idols,” calling the holy icons “idols.” And he also said to him with a threat: “Your king will soon lose not only his kingdom but also his life, if he does not stop bowing to idols.”

Returning, the envoy told this to the king, and threw him into confusion.
