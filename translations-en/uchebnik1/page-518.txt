The wise elder with gray hair, who bore the bird name Kiknos (which means Swan) and ceaselessly glorified God, urged Vardan not to think of the royal throne. “Otherwise,” he said, “you will lose your eyes, as all the others have.”

When he saw that Vardan was downcast, the elder again called out to the commander: “You, O glorious general, will not receive the kingdom, although you have tens of thousands of soldiers under your authority. Others who are under your command will gird themselves with the royal belt. There will be three men, and their names are Leo, Thomas, and Michael. Of them Leo will first receive power; after him the scepter will pass to Michael. Thomas, the third, will dare and will be very zealous in his intention to seize the royal throne, but he will not be able to bring his intention to completion, and he will receive the extinguishing of his eyes’ sight.” And all that Vardan heard, all that the wondrous elder Kiknos foretold like a trumpet moved by the Spirit, came to pass in due time.

Vardan, fearing God and not wishing to allow civil strife, thought: “Will bloodshed among Christians begin because of me?” He wrote to Emperor Nikephoros, and when the latter promised not to persecute him, at night, secretly from people, he hastened to the monastery of Heraclius to take monastic vows as quickly as possible.

But when Vardan was sailing on the imperial boat sent for him to the first island, Proti, to the monastery which he himself had established in former times, certain men, Lycaonians—whom it would be more accurate to call Wolf-people (that is, wolf-natured)—blinded him, allegedly without the emperor’s knowledge. The emperor, showing everyone that he was overcome with great grief, fervently swore that he was not guilty of what had been done, for it is said: “Never torture a Christian, and do not mutilate his limbs,” and that Vardan had been pious and merciful to his neighbors.

In the third year of Nikephoros’s reign, the Saracen prince set out against him with a great army and came to Dirilia. Then Nikephoros conveyed through his chief adviser these words to their prince: “Why do you rejoice in unrighteousness and human bloodshed? Do you not have enough, so as to go back to your own land? Why do you transgress the ordinance of the righteous? Which of the divine prophets or teachers commanded such a thing? Was it not Bohmit, your prophet, who enjoined you: ‘Receive a Christian as a brother and love him’? Does the Creator of all things and Teacher rejoice in the unrighteous bloodshed of Christians or Muslims? No! But He says: ‘Avoid silver, gold, and other superfluities; do not inflict needless wrongs on one who does not wrong you.’ We are ready to give you now whatever you wish, for we have much wealth in our holy land.
