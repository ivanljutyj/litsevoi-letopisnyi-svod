The Fortieth Reign: Michael Rhangabe, who reigned in Constantinople

After Staurakios, Michael Rhangabe, the son-in-law of Nikephoros, reigned for one year and nine months. He was adorned with all manner of virtues, and then a time of prosperity came for Byzantium; days arrived unstained by the dark cloud of violence and murder. Michael was a quiet and gentle ruler; he did not thirst for bloodshed but rejoiced in every good deed, and he was truly a blessed protection for the state, which, like a fertile pasture or a paradise garden, was nourished by life-giving moisture. But he became like a rosebud that has fallen prematurely, and as in the place of a withered flower a weed sprout rises and from it grows a thorny bramble, so the beast-named Leo the Armenian, destroyer of Christ’s flock, seized power. Having learned the torturer’s intentions, the pious Michael, preferring Christ’s commandments to an earthly kingdom and fulfilling them, of his own will left the royal throne desired by many and took up the silent monastic life.

The torturer seized power without difficulty. Michael stood before him like the beast castor, or beaver, from the pagan parable. When this beast is hunted with dogs, with the aim of taking from it the generative organs that are used as medicine in healing many diseases, the castor, knowing the reason for the pursuit and understanding the inevitability of the hunters’ nets, bites off its organs and throws them to the pursuers, and so saves itself. Thus Michael too, understanding that his reign was the cause of enmity and bloodshed, desired the salvation of his soul more than earthly power. The fierce serpent, wishing to destroy all Michael’s line, castrated his son Niketas, so that no child might be born from him. And this could not be prevented. The son of Michael Rhangabe was castrated and in monasticism was named Ignatius. Later he became archbishop of New Rome, Constantinople.
