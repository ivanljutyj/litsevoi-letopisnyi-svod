Vespasian, to whom power came not without God's providence, was granted by the justice of fate to be autocrat.

And Vespasian remembered the signs and the prophecy of Josephus, when, while Nero was still alive, he dared to call Vespasian emperor. And the commander was ashamed that he held this man in chains. And Vespasian summoned Mucianus and other commanders and told them how Josephus had prophesied to him, and said: “Then I thought he lied out of fear. Now time has shown that he was right, and everything he foretold has come to pass. It is monstrous to keep in captivity and in chains one who foretold my power and declared the Divine will.” And Vespasian summoned Josephus and ordered him to be unshackled. But Titus said to him: “It would be well, father, to remove the shame from Josephus as well. If we do not unfasten his chains but cut them through, then he will be as if he had never been chained.” And Vespasian ordered it so, and Josephus’s chains were cut through. And Josephus received this honor, and they began to believe what he said about the future.

And Vespasian sent Mucianus to Italy and gave him many horsemen and foot soldiers. But Mucianus feared to go by sea in the winter days and led his soldiers by land through Cappadocia and Phrygia.

Then also Antonius, a supporter of Vespasian, with the third legion from Moesia took up arms against Vitellius.

And Vitellius sent Caecina against Antonius, because he hoped in him as the conqueror of Otho. And Caecina set out quickly from Rome and met Antonius in Gaul, and saw the multitude of the enemy, and also the wisdom and strength of the commanders, and did not dare fight them. But Caecina judged that flight was terrible, and began to contemplate betrayal. And he gathered his centurions and commanders of thousands, and urged them to submit to Antonius, and reproached Vitellius, and extolled the strength of Vespasian, and said: “It is better to join Vespasian and not expose ourselves to danger than in madness to test the strength of arms. Vespasian can subdue all even without us. But Vitellius even with us will not save himself.” Caecina said much of this and persuaded them, and they joined Antonius.

That same night remorse fell upon Caecina’s soldiers for having betrayed Vitellius and gone over to the side of Antonius and Vespasian, and they feared Vitellius, who had sent them, who, if he won, would kill them. And the soldiers drew their swords and rushed at Caecina to kill him. And they would have killed him if the centurions had not begged them to spare Caecina. But the soldiers listened to the centurions and did not kill Caecina, but shackled him as a traitor and sent him to Vitellius.
