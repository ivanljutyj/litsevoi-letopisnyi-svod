In the same year Maximian Galerius reigned together with Constantine, with two Caesars appointed as emperors by the army; and they helped one another to labor zealously in the persecution of Christians.

On the War with Maxentius

Maxentius the torturer, son of Maximian Herculius and brother of Theodora, wife of Constantius, father of Constantine the Great, who was paternal uncle to Dalmatius and Valerian, was the most cunning of all the Roman rulers who had been before him. Having unlawfully made himself emperor in Rome, he raged and vented malice, robbed and extorted, and did the like. And many better than he, chosen by the council of elders, he put to death as Christians, while other men’s wives who had become free he subjected to unclean and foul adultery, giving them over to corruption and outrage, and compelled others by tortures. Hearing this and much else, the blessed Emperor Constantine, having also made an agreement with the Romans not to leave unpunished the death of the emperors at the hands of the cruel torturer and beast-like murderer, was greatly enraged and held a funeral feast for his good servants and God-loving men, strangers to falsehood. And many times by letters he exhorted Maxentius to cease doing evil, but he did not listen to him.

Constantine ordered them to take up arms, so that in this way the suffering Romans, who had no help, might be delivered from these evils—in other words, to save people. Hearing of this, however, the impious Maxentius did not cease doing evil. In the East, Maximian Galerius did even more bitter and much evil, having received East and West as an inheritance, and then raised war and persecution against the Christians. And in Rome the council of elders appointed Caesar Licinius as emperor. He was chosen for his submissiveness, agreeing in everything with his brother-in-law, the pious Constantine; he was the husband of Constantine’s sister and played the hypocrite, deceiving. And the pious Severus, beaten by the soldiers in Rome before everyone’s eyes, died.

Then Saint Constantine, seeing the inactivity and silence of the Roman authorities and hearing of the sufferings of Rome from the malice of Maxentius, and in the East from Maximian Galerius, was distressed by his own indecision in punishing the torturers.

Maxentius the impious, learning that the Christ-loving Constantine had entered Italy, with great boldness began to gather the Roman soldiers, and blocking the river with ships, he stopped its flow near the Milvian Bridge and took up arms against Constantine the Great.
