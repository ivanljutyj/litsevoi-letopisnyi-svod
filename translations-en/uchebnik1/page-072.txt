Evaratus of Cos, of whom we must write. He was a friend of Alexander and came at the same time as Eurycles. When the king inquired about the slander brought against Aristobulus and Alexander, Evaratus swore great oaths that he had heard nothing from the young men of what they were accused. Yet this testimony brought no benefit to the unfortunate brothers. For Herod listened only to the slanderers, and whoever was at peace and of one mind with him and condemned the young men became a good friend to him. Salome, Herod’s sister, also joined in his cruelty.

Aristobulus, wishing to draw Salome, his mother-in-law, into his misfortune, sent word to her to beware the king, for he was plotting to kill her, since the king had received news that Salome wished to marry the Arab Syllaeus, Herod’s enemy, and had previously passed royal secrets to Syllaeus. This warning of Aristobulus stirred up a storm that fell upon him and his brother.

For Salome went to the king and told him of Aristobulus’s warning. Then Herod could not endure it and put both sons in chains, separated them, and sent to Caesar the commander Volumnius and his friend Olympus with a letter about these events.

Herod’s envoys sailed to Rome and delivered the letter to Caesar. And Caesar was deeply grieved for the young men, but he judged it wrong to take from a father his authority over his children, and wrote back to Herod: “I confirm your right to be master over your children, but you will do well if you bring the accused before a general court made up of your kinsmen and my nobles, and they together examine the case and reach a decision. If it appears that the young men are guilty and truly plotted against you, they should be put to death. But if they only contemplated flight, they deserve a milder punishment.”

Herod followed this advice and came to the city of Berytus, appointed by Caesar, and assembled a general court there. The president of the court, by Caesar’s command, was Saturninus; next sat Pedanius and Volumnius; then the king’s kinsmen and all the rulers of the land of Syria, except King Archelaus. For Archelaus was the father-in-law of Alexander, and Herod feared him. Herod did not bring his sons themselves to the court, for he well knew that the mere sight of them would stir pity in all, and that if they were allowed to speak, Alexander would soon be pardoned. Therefore he kept them in Platana, a Sidonian village. Then the king rose and began to accuse his sons as though they stood before him. Herod wished to prove what he accused his sons of—that they had plotted against him—but he could not do so, since he had no reliable witnesses.
