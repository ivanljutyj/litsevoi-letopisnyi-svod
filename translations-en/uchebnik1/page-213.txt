The Jews slaughtered the Romans in battle; some of those who had only climbed up and had not yet raised a shield, the Jews overtook and cut down. Other Jews pushed the ladders away from the walls and threw them down.

The Romans in some places raised the imperial standards. The Jews rushed forward and made a fierce fight, trying to seize the standards. The Romans considered the loss of a standard a great disgrace and did not retreat, and fought until all had perished.

The Jews seized the standards and killed those who had climbed onto the walls, and boasted as if they had killed or captured the emperor himself. But none of the Romans died without vengeance; each killed first and only then was killed.

Titus, seeing that his mercy toward the temple was harming his soldiers, ordered the gates to be set on fire. Then Ananus of Emmaus and Archelaus, son of Magadates, deserted to him in the hope of obtaining pardon from Titus, since they had crossed over when the Jews were prevailing. Titus thought they had come with deceit, for he knew the Jews’ inflexibility, and he already wanted to kill them, and said: “You have come under compulsion, not of your own free will, and are unworthy of salvation, since you deserted when you saw your fatherland ablaze.” But for the sake of his given word Titus restrained his anger and pardoned them, yet he did not grant them terms equal to those in which the other deserters remained.

The soldiers set fire to the silver gates, and when the silver melted and ran, the flame seized the boards, and from there unexpectedly spread along the passages. The Jews saw the fire around them, and their bodies and souls weakened, and from terror no one even tried to put out the fire, but they stood in one place and watched.

Then, greatly grieved, the Jews came to their senses, but did not become reasonable; rather they became even more enraged at the Romans. All that day and all night the Romans set fire to houses and covered passages.

On the next day Titus sent to extinguish the fire and level to the ground what had been burned, and to widen the path for the army. And Titus summoned six of the chief commanders: Tiberius Alexander, and Sextus Cerealis, and Larcius Lepidus, and Titus Phrygius, and Fronto Aeternius, and Marcus Antonius Julianus, governor of Judea; and Titus consulted with them about the temple.

Some of these commanders said: “By the law of war it is fitting to deal with the temple, for the rebellion among the Jews will not cease while the temple stands, to which they gather from everywhere.” Others of the commanders said: “If the Jews stop and do not take up arms in the temple, then it is fitting to preserve the temple.
