Vespasian, however, constantly helped his perishing soldiers and saw buildings falling upon them, and he suffered grievously, and withdrawing little by little from his fortification, he went toward the mountain as though he had forgotten everything, and found himself among the enemies with very few soldiers. And his son was not with him there. And Vespasian wanted to flee, but he thought that flight would be dangerous, and that it was not fitting for him.

Then Vespasian remembered the trials he had endured since the days of his youth, and this gave him strength, and he stood firm with his close soldiers, and prepared for battle, fearing neither the multitude of enemies nor the missiles that they hurled at him. And Vespasian stood with his fighters and beat back the enemies pressing upon him from above, until the townsmen realized that his courage was not human but God-given.

And the townsmen slackened their attack. Then Vespasian began to withdraw calmly, not showing his back, until he had gone beyond the city walls.

In that battle eleven thousand Romans fell, and also those who aided them. Among them the centurion Ebutius also fell, a soldier who had earlier shown himself strong and brave everywhere, and in this his last battle displayed his courage and did much harm to the Jews.

Another centurion, named Gallus, and ten soldiers with him, in the confusion that arose entered a certain house and hid in the cellar. Later the owners came and began to sup, and in their conversation all that the people in the city were thinking came out. The Romans with the centurion Gallus lay under the floor and heard. When the owners were asleep at night, the Romans came out, killed them all, and fled to their camp.

On courage, and also that one should not boast when one has defeated the enemy

Vespasian saw that his soldiers were greatly dejected, since they had not yet known such a foolish and such a disastrous defeat. They were also ashamed and disgraced that they had left their commander in the heat of battle. Vespasian summoned his soldiers to comfort them, and did not shame them for having left him and fled. And Vespasian said to his soldiers: "It is fitting for us to bear misfortune courageously. Such is the nature and custom of war, that nothing in it stands in one place, but all changes. Victory is not granted without the shedding of blood. Fortune is inconstant, passing from some to others.
