The so-called idria often kills this crocodile; it lives in the same river and has a dog’s appearance, and is such in form and habits. For the crocodile opens its mouth while resting, and the idria, having rolled itself in dung, that is, in mud, enters through its mouth into its belly, and tears open its hind passage, and quickly comes out, leaving the crocodile dead. But let us return to what has been said up to now and go on further.

When Khosrow, king of Persia, was driven from his kingdom, Maurice sent Narses the general to restore Khosrow to the throne. Narses went and defeated Bahram, Khosrow’s opponent, and killed many of the Persians, and took six thousand alive, and brought them to Maurice. On the Turks. And the Turks who were among them had crosses inscribed on their foreheads. And they asked them: “What is this sign on you?” They said: “When we were in our dwellings, a great deadly pestilence befell us. The Christians who were among us made these signs, and immediately the plague ceased.” And Khosrow again received the kingdom in Persia. Then Patriarch John the Faster died, and Kyriakos became patriarch in his place.

Maurice the emperor made Philippicus his son-in-law, marrying him to his sister—a man valiant, nobly bold, brave, and rich, seething with intelligence and radiant with victories, “who had but lately left Old Rome.”

When he came to Constantinople, having destroyed the unrest in Persia, Maurice suspected this Philippicus of intending to seize the kingdom from him, because Maurice had heard from certain lawless soothsayers some old-womanish nonsense that his life and rule would be taken from him by someone having the letter fert in his name.

Then the Khagan came against the guards of the Greek borders; the Khagan was king of the midday Scythians, that is, the southern.

And the Khagan took fortified cities and laid waste the surroundings of the Ister, that is, the Danube, and showed the river streams mixed with blood.

Maurice the emperor ordered the general to give his soldiers over to the enemy, because of their opposition and disobedience, and the barbarians seized twelve thousand of Maurice’s soldiers.

The nobles asked the emperor for permission to ransom them, and, having listened to them, the emperor sent envoys to the barbarian prince, that he should release as many of Maurice’s soldiers as he had seized.
