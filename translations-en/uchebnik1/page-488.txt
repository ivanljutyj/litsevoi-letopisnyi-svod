The 30th Reign, of Philippicus, also called Bardanes, who reigned in Constantinople

After Justinian reigned Philippicus, also called Bardanes, for two years, installed by the soldiers in Cherson. Justinian was in turmoil because his soldiers were delaying their return from Cherson.

Then Justinian himself went overland to Sinope, and led many soldiers with him, both mounted and foot. And Justinian saw ships and war galleys coming swiftly, and was moved in mind and wounded in soul, and turned back, and mounted a swift horse, flying and swift-footed, and, if it were possible, begged the horse to become winged. But the favorable wind caused the sea ships to reach Constantinople sooner than Justinian.

Thus Philippicus with soldiers, having anticipated Justinian, took the reigning city and entered into imperial rights.

The noseless emperor tarried, that is, was late, going along impassable paths, and arrayed himself with his army, and came to the high Damatrian mountains. Then Philippicus sent his commanders to fight with Justinian.

The commanders of Philippicus stole away—that is, enticed with false promises—the military force that was with Justinian, and seized the thrice-wretched Justinian, and slaughtered him like a voiceless ox at the manger, and, surrounding him, with great onslaught poured out their fury upon him, like a hungry lion when it tastes the sinews of a deer.

They caught Justinian’s son Tiberius as he ran to the Church of the Mother of God, and tore him away from the altar—he was still small—and, alas, struck the sufferer against the threshold like a puppy, and broke the joints of his head like wheat, and his brain flowed onto the floor; this was a terrifying sight. So well and wisely did the ancients say: “Do nothing beyond measure.” Justinian kindled a furnace of wrath against the Chersonites “seven times seven,” and through this kindled in his slaves a flame of hatred against himself. It is said in Scripture: “God hates exceedingly evil undertakings.”

Emperor Philippicus kept in his heart impiety and the seeds of manifold malice, which did not escape God’s all-seeing eyes; therefore Philippicus’s eyes were gouged out while he slept, by the command of Theodore and George, who were of patrician rank.

The 31st Reign, of Artemius, also called Anastasius, who reigned in Constantinople

After Philippicus reigned Artemius, also called Anastasius, for two years. That Artemius blinded Theodore and George, who had blinded Philippicus, and sent them into exile in Thessalonica.
