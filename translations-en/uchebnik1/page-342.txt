And Valens ordered all those who were in the ship to be burned, and they all burned in the ship, and the ship floated intact even as far as the village of Dakizis, and there it finally broke apart.

Valens in every way laid waste the Church and went to Caesarea, plotting evil against Basil the Great. But Basil put Valens to shame with wise words and raised his dead son, as Gregory the Theologian reports.

Demosthenes, one of Valens’s associates, met Basil for a conversation and spoke like a barbarian; and the hierarch smiled slightly and said: “So we have seen Demosthenes illiterate.” The Goths rose up strongly against Valens, and Valens asked the divine Valentinian, his brother, to send him soldiers for help. But he did not give him soldiers, but reviled him as a wicked heretic, and Valentinian said to Valens: “It is not fitting for a man to raise rebellion against God.”

On the Killing of the Monks

At that time, when 46 years of Athanasius’s episcopate had passed, having accomplished many labors and attained many crowns of abstinence, he fell silent, and Peter took his throne, who, as was said before, shared sorrows with Athanasius.

Luke the bishop, an adherent of the heresy of Arius, rushed with zeal like a wolf against sheep. Peter fled to Rome, and this Luke walked through human blood, so that it seemed even the appearance of service would not be preserved. At the beginning of his accession to the throne there was such great defilement of church virgins who lived in continence as cannot be remembered even in the Hellenic persecution.

Not only was this done, but the deserts were also laid waste. Luke organized persecutions against the venerable and the silent, gathered 3000 people together, and sent them into the desert to fight the monks. And the monks saw the worldly soldiers, and the sent adversaries cut down the bowed monastic necks, and nothing else was heard from the monks except: “O friend, be bold and do what you came for.”

The Miracle of the Lame Man

At that time the monk fathers labored—elders by life and by order—Macarius, Isidore, and another Macarius, and Ammon, and Heraclius, and the disciples of Anthony in Egypt, mostly in the desert places of Nitria.
