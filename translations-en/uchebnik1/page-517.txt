At night the logothete of the genikon (chief treasurer), patrikios Nikephoros, appeared with the conspirators at the Palace of Eleutherios. He approached the Bronze Gates of the palace and, telling the guard that he had come by the empress's command, entered the palace with candles and weapons.

At once he sent from there throughout the whole city to proclaim himself emperor. In the morning Irene found herself locked in her chambers, and Nikephoros, before all the people, was proclaimed emperor by Patriarch Tarasios.

Reign 38 of Nikephoros Genikos, who reigned in Constantinople

After Irene, Nikephoros reigned for eight and a half years. This murderer was of evil nature, evil-tongued, and so fond of gold that gold made him its slave. He who exiled Irene to Selovensky Island was like King Midas, who once possessed Phrygia, in his love of gold. He sold city ranks and offices; he rewarded not merit but wealth, hypocritically concealing his insatiable passion for money beneath the mask of feigned kindness and justice. And gold, which he fancied he saw everywhere, he would sell almost in his sleep.

Thus he usually appeared before people complaining and dejected. But if someone brought gold, his eyes lit up with joy, and the gloom of complaint vanished from his face. And in order to seek out and obtain gold anywhere, he set cunning nets and employed every kind of trick.

O evil passion of love of silver! Under the pretext of the necessity of restoring the dilapidated city walls, he imposed exorbitant taxes on the Greeks.

When gold, silver, or gold-woven vestments came into his hands, he would no longer let them go. As it is said, "Many are the traces of those who bring their treasures in, but the traces of those who carry them out are not found." He deprived the soldiers of their pay, as a result of which unrest and murmuring of discontent began in the army.

And all the multitude of soldiers flocked to a commander named Bardanes, and, having told him their need, they began to beg him to become emperor. He refused in every way, but at last, yielding, compelled by his soldiers, he marched on Korsopolis and besieged it for eight days, but without taking the city he returned to Magnia.

The God-fearing Bardanes could not fail to understand how inconstant and fleeting are honors and power, and he sought counsel from a certain elder who, by the grace of God, had acquired the gift of clairvoyance, and revealed to him his secret design and the hopes of the people.
