We shall tell of this further. The tower called the Tower of Antonia was built between two covered passages of the temple, on a high rock, 50 cubits in height. King Herod built this tower, and he covered the rock with marble slabs from its foot to its summit, for beauty and utility, so that it was impossible either to enter or to leave. And before the tower Herod built a wall three cubits high, and then he built the tower to a height of 40 cubits. The inner space of the tower was occupied by the royal palace, defended, beautiful, possessing everything needful, with passages, and large baths, and broad houses for soldiers. And since everything necessary was in the Tower of Antonia, it resembled a fortified city. And everything in it was beautiful and varied, as in a royal dwelling. In appearance it was a tower at whose corners were four other towers; three of them rose to 50 cubits, while the fourth, on the eastern side, was 70 cubits high. So the temple could be seen from the Tower of Antonia, and Roman guards with weapons sat in it on feast days and watched that there should be no disorder among the people. The temple fortress was to Jerusalem like an inner citadel. The Tower of Antonia was the highest and strongest in the temple fortress. In the Upper City, Herod’s palace was a separate inner citadel. Bezetha was separated from the Tower of Antonia and looked toward the temple from the north. Of the city and its walls, as far as I was able, I have told.

The opponents of the Romans in Jerusalem were as follows: Simon Niger had 15,000, and Idumeans, and 60 commanders; John, that is Johanan, had 6,000, and 20 commanders, and 2,600 Zealots; and the common people were countless in number. When the rebels fought among themselves, as we said, the peaceful people became prey for both bands of rebels.

Whoever among the rebels prevailed took what he wanted. Simon Niger seized the Upper City and the great walls as far as the Kidron and Siloam, and the Lower City, and the well. John, that is Johanan, seized the temple fortress, and Ophel, and the surrounding area. In the middle they burned the place and made it ready for battles.

For their struggle did not cease even when the Romans surrounded the city. They came to their senses only a little when the Romans first arrived, but then they again began to feud with one another and did everything that the enemy wanted and expected of them. The rebels did not experience such sufferings from the enemy himself as from one another. And for the city there was no greater misfortune than enmity within the city. Even before its fall Jerusalem was weakened, and the Romans, when they took the city and received glory, said that the rebellion had conquered the city for them, while they had defeated the rebellion, which was much stronger than the walls.
