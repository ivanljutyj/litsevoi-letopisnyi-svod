And when the Germans had already joined with the Gauls and taken up arms, then Vespasian, by God's providence, sent a letter to Petilius Cerialis, ordering him to go to Britain and govern there. And Cerialis went where he was commanded, and learned that the Germans were mustering for a swift war, and fought with them for a long time, and killed many of them, and forced them to abandon their insolence and return to reason and submission. If Cerialis had not intervened, the Germans would not have had long to wait for punishment even then, for Domitian, the second son of Vespasian, having learned of everything, did not delay. When news came to him of the insolence of the Germans, Domitian did not flinch or fear to undertake such a difficult matter, although he was still very young in age. For he had in his heart his father's courage, and he was perfectly skilled in the art of war, and he quickly rode against the barbarians. But the Germans, learning of Domitian's approach, sent to him with entreaty and agreed to all that he might command, if only he would not destroy them in their folly.

Domitian imposed on them their former burdens and restored order, and returned to Rome with glory and honor, with a victory great beyond his years, which worthily adorned his father's old age.

In those same days Rome experienced other disturbances. The so-called Sarmatians secretly crossed the river Ister, also called the Danube, and with great ferocity, like a storm, suddenly attacked the Roman guards, and killed many of them, including Fonteius Agrippa, the commander of the guards. And the Sarmatians fought hard with the Romans, and cut them down, and ravaged all the Roman lands there.

Vespasian, having learned what had happened, with great haste sent his commander Rubrius Gallus to take vengeance on the Sarmatians, and he killed many of them, while the survivors fled in fear to their own country. The Roman commander, having ended the war, strengthened that place with a large number of guards, so that the barbarians could no longer cross the Danube.
