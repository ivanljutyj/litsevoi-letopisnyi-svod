After this, likewise at the instigation of Bardas, he also removed his mother Theodora from the palace and sent her to that same monastery. As she left the palace, Empress Theodora left in the sacristy 1090 gold kentenaria (one kentenarion corresponds to 150 litrai, and a litra in weight equals half a dvugrivenka), as well as a great quantity of silver and precious garments. And Michael squandered all this with his dissolute playfellows.

After this Michael reigned 10 years. He appointed Basil to the place of the deceased chief saddler, and made Bardas, his uncle, curopalates, that is, commander of the palace guard.

Bardas's son, named Antigonus, Emperor Michael confirmed in the office of domestic of the Schools (commander of the corps of the capital troops). Another of his sons he married to Bardas's concubine and set him as great strategos in the western regions of the empire, where he died.

After no long time, on the Wednesday of Bright Week, Emperor Michael bestowed on Bardas, his uncle, the title of Caesar, and he, having received power, became co-ruler with Michael. Michael entrusted to him the government of the city.

Amer, having reached the Pisidian city, ravaged the coastal lands and returned back without waiting for the arrival of the Greek troops.

On the Adultery of Caesar Bardas

When the divine Methodius departed to the Lord, Saint Ignatius, son of Emperor Michael Rhangabe, who had been castrated by Leo the Armenian, was raised to the patriarchal throne.

Caesar Bardas not only gradually took the empire into his hands, but even reached such a grave lawlessness as neither a Scythian nor a Persian nor a pagan would have dared. Openly, as if with a lawful wife, he began to live with the wife of his son. O the power of lustful passion! This lawlessness became known to the great Ignatius, Patriarch of New Rome, for news of this abomination went through the whole empire: it was told at every crossroads and in every house.

The holy hierarch sternly denounced Caesar Bardas, terrified him with fear of future torments, and in every way urged him to cut off the passion. But true is Solomon's proverb, which forbids rebuking fools, for rebukes are like calluses to the ungodly and, like arrows, wound their evil hearts. So the Caesar, angered by the words of the holy Ignatius with which he vexed him, not only was not corrected by his instructions, but also harbored enmity toward the holy hierarch, just as those covered with scabs, when treated by some benefactor, not only run away unable to bear the pain, but even grow angry with him.
