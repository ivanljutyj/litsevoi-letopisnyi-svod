Romanus the patrician, who was then drungarius, was sent with all the naval forces to the river called the Danube to aid Leo Phocas, while John, called Voga, was sent to bring the Pechenegs, as has been said. Drungarius Romanus was ordered to ferry them across to aid Leo Phocas against the Bulgars.

Romanus and John Voga began to argue and quarrel with each other. Seeing how they were pursuing strife among themselves, the Pechenegs resisted and went home.

When the war ended and Romanus and Voga returned to Constantinople, it recoiled upon them and brought Drungarius Romanus into such misfortune that the court sentenced him to blinding for negligence, and moreover for villainy: having failed to ferry the Pechenegs across, he fled hastily and did not take aboard the ships the Greeks who were fleeing for safety.

And this would have been done to him had Constantine the patrician, nicknamed Gongyla, and Stephen the magistros, who enjoyed great favor with the empress, not talked him out of the sentence.

When the Bulgars grew proud from victory and came in war as far as Constantinople, Leo, domestikos of the Scholai, John the hetaireiarch, and Nicholas, son of Doukas, went out against the Bulgars into the Thracian country, to the so-called Katasyrtes.

At night the Bulgars unexpectedly attacked them; the domestikos fled, Nicholas, son of Doukas, was killed, and many others with him.

Now Theodore, tutor of Emperor Constantine, seeing that Constantine the parakoimomenos was drawing the empire toward Leo, his son-in-law, suggested to Emperor Constantine that he take Drungarius Romanus, as a former servant of his father and one well-disposed toward him, so that he might be with him and guard him, and when necessary be his comrade-in-arms and helper. Many times this was said to Romanus, but he refused.

Then Emperor Constantine wrote a letter with his own hand and, confirming it with his signature, sent it to him. And Romanus, having received this letter, was compelled, as was proper, to act against Constantine the parakoimomenos and his relatives.

And rumors about this spread, and Constantine the parakoimomenos pressed Romanus to withdraw with his ships. Since Romanus, being busy preparing and outfitting the ships, pleaded that “it is impossible for me to sail away from the city, because the soldiers have not received their pay,” Constantine the parakoimomenos came to force him to withdraw. Romanus, feigning submission to the parakoimomenos, said: “I will gladly carry out what is commanded.”
