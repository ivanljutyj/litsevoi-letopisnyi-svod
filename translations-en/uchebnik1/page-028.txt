This same Augustus traversed the whole land of Europe, and came from Byzantium to Chalcedon, a city in Asia. And he immediately appointed a prince in Bithynia from his own men, named Dus, who ruled that land of Dinar; Caesar, his uncle, had appointed him earlier. Previously Bithynia had princes, but afterward Pompey the Great received rule in that Bithynia when Nicomedes, its governor, died; he was of Macedonian stock, and as he died he left Bithynia to the Romans.

Galatia this Augustus subdued, having defeated Deiotarus, its tetrarch. And Augustus enclosed the settlement called Arsini and made it a city, which he named Ancyra, that is, anchor, since the Pontic Sea is situated on both sides of it. He sacrificed the maiden Gregoria for the purification of the city, and established a province, and also admitted Gallic warriors into it to guard that land and city, and named that land, the province, and the river Gallis after them. Likewise Lydia and Pamphylia: both these provinces were subject to Deiotarus, and Augustus received them and made them subject to Rome, and sent there Curio, his commander, with a great force. And Phrygia Pacatiana, which was in the same province, he made subject to Rome, sending Pacatianus, his commander, with a force. And Lycaonia Augustus subordinated to Rome; it was ruled by Lycaon, son of Capys, who attached himself to Augustus; and Augustus entrusted his possessions to Lycaon, and took him with him against Egypt. Syria and Cilicia had been subordinated to Rome earlier, under Caesar Julius. And Augustus came to Syria, and entered Great Antioch, and confirmed the victory that he had won over Antony the tyrant and over Cleopatra, with Agrippa, his son-in-law. This Agrippa loved the city of Antioch, and built a bath outside the city near the mountain, finding there a spring which he named after himself—Agrippius. This same Agrippa also built and added a second tier above the first to the theater building in Antioch, for the sake of the great multitude of people.

This same Agrippa departed from there, and Augustus came to Laodicea, a city in Syria, and built in that city a very large theater, and also set up a marble statue of himself there. And he also built in the same city a great canopy on four pillars—previously the canopy was small—and adorned the pillars with marble, and made the enclosure still larger, and marked his victory in that city, and set on that four-gated structure a bronze statue with four horses. Augustus also subdued the land of Phoenicia, and sent commanders with aid, Lucalus and Ponton, who defeated Tigranes, who ruled that land, and made it a province.
