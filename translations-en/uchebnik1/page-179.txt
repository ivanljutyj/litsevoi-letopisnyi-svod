The second wall begins at the Gate of Gennath and goes to the Tower of Antonia, where it ends. The third wall goes from the Horse Tower to the Muzian Tower and ends at the tomb of Helena and the royal caves, where the tomb of Gnatheus was. With this new wall Agrippa enclosed the outer fortress, and it was called Bezetha, or the New City. But Agrippa did not finish this work, because he feared Emperor Claudius, lest he think that such a great structure had been undertaken so that the Romans could not take this city. If Agrippa had finished the walls as he began—for he built them of stones twenty cubits thick and ten wide—then this wall could neither be cut through with iron tools nor moved by “rams.” But Agrippa did not finish this work, as we said, fearing Roman suspicion. The Jews raised the wall to twenty-five cubits. The Temple was built on a high hill, on its level summit. Around the hill were great ravines. King Solomon built walls on the eastern side, and made an embankment, and on it covered passages. But on the other sides the Temple was unprotected. And people in later times kept filling in earth and leveled the hill, and widened it, and cut through the southern walls, and from there enclosed the lands adjacent to the Temple, and thus enclosed the hill all around. And the Jews labored beyond measure and accomplished a glorious work, on which long ages and the sacred treasure sent from the whole world to honor God were spent. And where there were deep places, there the foundation of the Temple was 300 cubits deep, made of stones forty cubits thick. A multitude of resources and the help of people produced such a building as surpassed all that was thought or spoken of. And only those who saw it believed, while those who heard could not believe. At the upper part of the Temple double covered passages were made, with stone columns in them, twenty-five cubits high, made from single pieces of white marble. The covering of the passages was of cedar boards, well planed and very beautiful by nature, so that one who looked at them could not quickly take his eyes from them: it seemed to be a painting made by hand, though it was not made by human hands. The walls ran in a circle for six versts. The uncovered courtyard was adorned with various stones and paved. From there was an ascent to the second Temple, along which ran a stone enclosure three cubits high and very pleasant in appearance, in which columns stood at equal distances from one another, and on them were inscriptions in Greek, Latin, and Hebrew, declaring the law of purity and that no foreigner should enter within.
