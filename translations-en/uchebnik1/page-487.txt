Others Justinian invited to dinner and to supper, that is, the evening meal, and by poison cut them off from this present life; others, saying that they were in a foreign land, that is, in exile, he in fact hanged. Such fear seized Constantine’s city that some fled to the barbarians.

Justinian also raged, that is, was angry, against those living within the bounds of Cherson in Pontus, for when he lived among them after his expulsion from the kingdom, sometimes they vexed him, sometimes they dishonored him, and wounded him with shameful arrows, and formed wicked plots to pierce his belly with his own sword, or to send him in chains to Apsimar. For this Justinian in his thoughts was grieved against them, as against barbarian-like men, and for this reason sent soldiers by sea against them, and commanded that the whole race together be slaughtered, since they had grievously offended him, and to spare neither women nor children, neither old nor young—so fiercely did he burn with wrath against them.

The suffering of the inhabitants of Cherson is worthy of weeping, and it moves the compassionate to lamentation, for it surpasses the much-sighing groans of Bethlehem; for there only infants were slaughtered by murderous hands, but here gray-haired elders, and youths in their prime, and fair-formed women, and maidens, and infants together were torn asunder. Wailing was everywhere and loud weeping. And the mountains cried out in answer to those weeping, and the shores roared with waves. Thus populous Cherson was suddenly turned into a Scythian desolation and became an all-deadly grave, and gates of death, and a many-gaping hellish womb that receives all.

When the fierce-hearted king heard that some mercy had been shown to the newborn infants, he was kindled with still greater fury against those who had spared them, and again sent a numerous army in ships, as if for battle, and with them Philippicus, whom they called Bardanes, the commander of the army; and Justinian commanded them to pierce, that is, slaughter, the newborn infants with weapons, and also those sucking milk, and to turn the once flourishing city into a field of desolation, and its streets into fields boiling with ears of grain. Such cruelty and fierce-heartedness became the cause of a new great evil for King Justinian, and again a storm of evil temptations rose against him, and he received an even greater avenging judgment.

For the military force sent against the Chersonites counted what had been commanded them as folly, and the soldiers did not wish to defile themselves with such great foul murder, and did not do what had been commanded them; but, moreover, they hated the king for his inhuman custom, as a fiercely wrathful tiger, as a blood-drinking wolf. Together with the soldiers, the remaining Chersonites also turned away from Justinian. And they made Philippicus king and lord. In the reign of this King Justinian, Gregory, bishop of Agrigento, son of Chariton and of Theodote his mother, labored.
