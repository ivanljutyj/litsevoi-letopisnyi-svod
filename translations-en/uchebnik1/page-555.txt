But fear suddenly seized the emperor, and he turned back. Not seeing the emperor beside him, Manuel turned back and fought his way to the emperor. Meanwhile Amer had run out of water and food, and in order to replenish them he withdrew to a place called Pidora. This saved the emperor and helped him avoid the hands of the enemy.

And again Amer set out against the Greeks. Then Petronas, strategos of the Thracian theme, brother of the empress, whom the emperor had placed at the head of the Greek forces in the east, gathered an army and hastened to meet Amer.

Meanwhile a certain deserter from the Greeks came to Amer and said: “Know, Amer, that the strategos Petronas, a kinsman of the emperor, has declared that he is going to war against you. Having gathered soldiers from various ranks, he is preparing to set out.”

Then Amer summoned his close advisers to give him counsel on what to do. And they proposed: “Let us return home, for the first victory is enough for us.”

But Amer did not listen to them and set out against Petronas. Soon his army was routed, and he himself perished by the sword.

Only his son, with a few soldiers, managed to cross the river Alia and flee, but in the region of Charsianon they were overtaken by the general Machera, called in our tongue Knifeman, and he took them all captive.

He brought Amer’s son to Constantinople and displayed him on the hippodrome for all to see as a military trophy. From that time, after Amer’s killing, peace reigned in the east.

Some, however, said that Bardas, having won such a victory with his brother, would now set his mind on seizing sole power. For the time being, however, caring for learning, he gathered the most learned men from everywhere and established a school in the Magnaura Hall of the imperial palace. In it various sciences were to be studied. To teach philosophy, Caesar Bardas invited Leo the Philosopher; geometry, his pupil Theodore; the law of the stars, that is, astronomy, Theogidisius; and grammar, Cometas.

Emperor Michael at this time built a stable for his horses, adorned it with marble, and brought water into it to water them. When the construction was completed, the emperor summoned an official named Peter, who was in charge of distributing alms, a shrewd and sensible man, so that he might admire his creation, and, expecting praise from him, said: “I have arranged all this to leave a memory of myself forever.” To this Peter replied: “Emperor Justinian accomplished what was beyond human strength—he raised Hagia Sophia and spent the entire treasury on its adornment, and he is not much remembered. But you, emperor, have built a receptacle of filth, and by this you hope to leave a memory of yourself?”
