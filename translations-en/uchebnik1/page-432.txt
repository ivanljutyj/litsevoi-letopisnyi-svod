Maurice desired this, overcome by love of money and hatred for his disobedient soldiers; neither Scythian savagery, nor the wondrous striving of the barbarian kagan, nor the wailing and tears of the captives bent Maurice’s obduracy.

The kagan sent again to Maurice, but he did not incline to mercy, and at this the kagan was enraged like a leopard and a tiger. O woe! By this refusal Maurice condemned such a multitude of people to bitter death, and—woe also to me!—the sword reaped such a multitude, and covered the face of the earth with corpses, and the fields were reddened with blood, as food for birds flying and for beasts. O gold, persecutor and most proud tormentor, destroyer of cities! O gold, you soften the cruel and harden the gentle; you open the tongue of the speechless and stop the mouths of the eloquent; by your glitter you catch hearts into desire. If you make even stones soft, who can escape your exceedingly strong power?

After this, having learned of the killing of so many men because of Emperor Maurice, all the people hated him, and reproached him for hardness of heart, and all began to revile and abuse him.

Then Maurice repented, came to himself, and, reflecting on the future Judgment, chose rather to receive retribution for his sins here than there at the dread Judgment.

And Maurice composed a letter of supplication and sent it to all the patriarchal sees, and to the ascetics in Jerusalem, and to the monasteries, and sent much gold, frankincense, and candles, and asked them to pray for him, that here he might receive retribution for his sin.

When Maurice prayed to God concerning the sin of the killings, he saw a dream like this: as though many people, standing before the icon of Christ that is above the Bronze Gates, were in the royal palace, and a Voice came from the icon, saying: “Give Maurice.” They took him and set him before the icon.

And the divine voice said: “And where do you wish, Maurice, that I repay you: here, or in the age to come?” Hearing this, Maurice trembled and answered: “O Lord, lover of mankind, here, and not there.” At once the Lord commanded to deliver him, and his wife, and his children, and all his associates, that is, his whole household, to Phocas the commander.

And the emperor awoke and quickly sent for his parakoimomenos, that is, his chief chamberlain, an officer who stood before him day and night, and ordered him to bring his son-in-law Philippicus urgently.

That Philippicus, seeing the parakoimomenos come at such an hour, bade farewell to his life and asked to receive communion. By this the illustrious and great one in battles was caught—O woe!—in a hostile net, that is, a snare, and instead of bright clothing was clothed in the black monastic garb.
