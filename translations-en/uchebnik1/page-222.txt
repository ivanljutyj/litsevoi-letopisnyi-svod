The regiments moved, terrified. And there was a cry and great confusion when such an army moved in disorder. The emperor by voice and hands signaled to the soldiers to put out the fire. But they did not hear his shouting, since they were deafened by the noise of battle, and did not heed the waving of his hands, for some were looking at the enemy—who would kill whom—while for others anger stopped their ears and turned away their eyes. The regiments kept coming, and neither orders nor threats nor entreaties could restrain their surge, but fury led them all, and all were seized by wrath. Crowding and jostling arose among the Romans, and many were trampled by one another. Many were pushed onto red-hot stones and coals and could no longer get out, and so burned. And those soldiers who stood by the church pretended not to hear the emperor’s command, and each of them encouraged the one next to him to kindle the fire further. The Jews rushed about, not knowing what to do. And everywhere there was fighting, and blood, and flight. People from the common folk, weak and unarmed, were killed wherever anyone found them. And at the altar a multitude of the dead gathered, and blood flowed like a river, and corpses floated in streams of blood.

The emperor, seeing that he could not restrain the army’s impulse and that the fire was prevailing, entered the temple with his commanders and saw the temple sanctuary, toward which they had so striven, and found all that was famous in it not only among the Jews but also among foreigners to be wondrous, most glorious, and beautiful. The flame had not yet penetrated inside, but was moving through the surrounding structures, and Titus thought that the sanctuary might still be saved from the fire; he rushed forward and began himself to put out the fire, and urged on the soldiers, and assigned Liberalis the centurion to watch over this. But the soldiers did not obey the order to stop the fire, for their wrath and hatred of the Jews overcame their respect for the emperor and fear of punishment. And the soldiers’ ardor blazed more fiercely than the flame. Many hoped to plunder and continued setting fires, thinking that the whole inner temple was full of wealth, since they saw that everything around was covered with gold.

When the emperor entered to forbid the soldiers to set fire to the inner temple, someone had already placed fire in the darkness at the doors of the inner temple, and at once flame appeared in the temple. And the commanders withdrew with the emperor, and no longer forbade anyone either to burn or to cut down. The temple was thus set alight contrary to the emperor’s wish. The destruction of this building deserves great lamentation, the most wondrous of all that people have seen or heard of. It was most wondrous both in its construction and in its size, and in its magnificence, and in its adornment, and in the glory of the sanctuary. Great consolation, however, will be found by him who looks upon the judgment of God, which cannot be avoided by those endowed with a soul or by soulless things, neither by buildings nor by places.
