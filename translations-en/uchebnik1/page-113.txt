For to become a slave is very bitter, and he who from the outset seeks to avoid this is right and worthy of praise. But he who first bowed under the yoke and then throws it off is a negligent slave, and he is not a lover of freedom but a troublemaker and a brigand. Then all should have risen and obtained freedom when Pompey came to our land. But our forefathers and first kings, who were stronger than we in soul, body, and wealth, could not resist even a small part of the Roman army. But you have inherited slavery from your fathers and now rise against the whole Roman state. Look at the Athenians—for the freedom of the Greeks they themselves gave their city to fire; and their enemy, proud Xerxes, sailed over land and walked over sea, and the sea’s abyss did not contain his ships, and his army was wider than the land; but the Athenians overcame him too, so that Xerxes fled with a single ship. And now those glorious Athenians are slaves of the Romans, and from Italy is governed the chief city that rules over all the Hellenic land. The Lacedaemonians came out victorious in the battles at Thermopylae and Plataea, and under King Agesilaus they took possession of Asia, but they too have now submitted and are under the same masters. The Macedonians, who knew Philip and Alexander and still remember how they ruled the world, have submitted and now bow to those to whom fortune has passed. And many other peoples, for all their striving for freedom, are humbled.

And you alone do not wish to be humbled before those to whom all submit. On what soldiers, then, do you rely? Or on what weapons? Where are your mighty ships, on which you can traverse all the Roman seas? Where are your riches? Or do you think you will fight against someone like the Egyptians or Arabs? Why do you not compare Roman strength with your own weakness? Your soldiers have many times been defeated in clashes with neighboring peoples. But no one in the whole universe has yet been able to defeat the Roman army: all the Euphrates on the eastern side submitted to them, and the northern river Danube, and in the south Libya, and in the west Gades. And beyond the ocean they subdued a new world, Britain, described by no one and unknown to anyone, and there the Romans carried their weapons. What will you say to this? Are you richer than the Gauls, stronger than the Germans, wiser than the Greeks? On what do you rely when you rise against the Romans? You say that slavery is bitter and shameful. But look at the Greeks—they are the noblest of all who live under the sun, and they possess so many lands, yet they submit to the six Roman rods, that is, to Roman authority. So too the Macedonians, and five thousand Asian cities—do they not bow to one ruler? A day would not suffice for me to name all who obey the Romans.
