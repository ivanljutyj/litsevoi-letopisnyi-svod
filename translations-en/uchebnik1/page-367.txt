And Arcadius prepared himself for this. It happened that Theophilus of Alexandria also came to Constantinople, a man who stood out among all men for his profundity and could learn hidden thoughts from the expression of a face. This Theophilus opposed the ordination of John more than anyone, and he tried to place on the patriarchate a certain man named Isidore, his presbyter, a host to strangers and a nourisher of strangers. I speak of Isidore not as the ascetic of Pelusium, who was first a disciple of Chrysostom, as some believe, but as the aforementioned host of strangers.

John was of good family from Antioch; he was the son of Secundus the general, and his mother’s name was Anthusa. John was very intelligent and eloquent. Libanius, his wise teacher, testified of him when those who came to him asked: “Whom will you appoint as teacher in your place?” And he answered them: “John, if the Christians do not take him from us.”

John was eighteen years old, and he left Libanius and was baptized by Meletius, archbishop of Antioch, and appointed reader, and he remained in the church three years.

And John went to a hegumen, a monk named Carterius, and lived with him four years. And Carterius made John a monk and instructed him in the Divine Scripture and in strict monastic life.

And John withdrew to a cave and remained in solitude two years; he loved silence and during those two years did not lie down either by night or by day. And his stomach became dead, and he contracted a disease from cold in his loins, and he could not be healed, and he came again to the church, and became a divine gain for the salvation of many. Then John was ordained deacon by Meletius and served at the altar two years, or three.

When his teacherly virtues shone forth, John was ordained presbyter by Flavian the bishop, and he was an ornament of the Church of Antioch; and this lasted three times four years. There John was honored with the dignity of the priesthood and was famed for his strict life. He was a strict ascetic and kept many vigils, and he loved silence greatly. Because of his chaste zeal John was bold, and extremely prone to anger, and fiercer than was proper, and bold with those who conversed with him, and unrestrained in speech without limit; he was so for instruction and for the benefit of his hearers, but it appeared as though he were some proud man, and those who knew him considered him arrogant.

And John, when he had been numbered among the bishops, addressed his hearers with even greater pride, for the correction of each of them and for their salvation, and John urged those who heard him to change in their deeds and words. John was not a flatterer; because of this they considered him proud.
