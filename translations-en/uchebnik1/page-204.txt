This calamity consisted in that the dead were carried outside the city and thrown into a pit. After this other defectors from among the nobles reported to Titus the number of the dead from the poor carried out through all the gates — 600,000 — while the number of the rest was unknown. Some of the dead were gathered and thrown into houses, and the houses were locked. And a measure of wheat was sold for a talent of gold. Later, when the city was blockaded and people could not go out to gather herbs, they came to such a pass that they began to seek cattle dung and pick it out. And that was food to them which previously they could not bear to look at. The Romans heard of such calamities and pitied them, but the rebels saw and felt no pity, and they waited until the end, until disaster should reach them. Their mind was blinded by God’s judgment, which brought all these misfortunes upon the city and upon those living in it.

Book 6, On the Conquest of Jerusalem

The calamities in Jerusalem increased more and more each day. The rebels grew ever more hardened amid the calamities. Famine, when the people had perished, was already destroying the rebels too. A multitude of corpses had accumulated within the city; their appearance was terrible, and an unbearable stench came from them. And when the rebels went out to fight, they walked over the bodies, and stepping on them they were not horrified, nor did they pity, nor did they fear the final oath, since they had outraged those lying there. They bloodied their hands with the murder of kin and then went against foreigners. They did not hope to conquer, but despaired of salvation, and blasphemed God because He delayed and did not soon send them the torment they deserved.

The Romans performed great labor, carried earth and timber, and raised siege mounds in 21 days. The Romans cut down, as was said before, the surrounding settlements for 120 versts from Jerusalem. It was pitiful to look upon all the surrounding land, which gardens and orchards had formerly adorned but which was now devastated and bare. And whoever had formerly seen the beauty of Judea and the magnificence of Jerusalem, if he now saw this devastation, would recognize nothing there, for war had destroyed all its beauty. If someone, even from those local places, had come from afar, standing at the city’s site he would begin to ask: “Where is the city?”

When the siege mounds were finished, fear fell upon both Romans and Jews. The Jews feared being conquered if they did not burn these structures, while the Romans did not hope to take the city if the mounds were burned. For no trees remained, and the soldiers were exhausted by much labor, and their souls were downcast by frequent failures. The Romans grieved over the city’s misfortune more than the townspeople did. And however hard the Romans fought the Jews, the latter did not soften, and each time the Romans’ hopes were destroyed.
