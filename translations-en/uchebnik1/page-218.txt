The Jews stabbed the Romans in battle; some who had only climbed up and had not yet managed to raise their shields, the Jews overtook and cut down. Other Jews pushed the ladders away from the walls and threw them down.

The Romans in some places raised up the imperial standards. The Jews rushed at them and made a fierce slaughter, trying to seize the standards. The Romans considered the loss of a standard a great disgrace and did not retreat, and fought until all had perished.

The Jews seized the standards and killed those who had climbed onto the walls, and boasted as though they had killed or taken captive the emperor himself. But none of the Romans died without vengeance, but first killed and then was killed.

Titus, seeing that his mercy toward the temple was harming his soldiers, ordered the gates to be set on fire. Then Ananus from Emmaus and Archelaus, son of Magadatus, defected to him in the hope of receiving pardon from Titus, since they defected at the time when the Jews were prevailing. Titus thought they had come with deceit, for he knew of the Jews' inflexibility, and he already wanted to kill them, and said: "You have come under compulsion, and not of your own free will, and are unworthy of salvation, since you defected when you saw that your fatherland was ablaze." But for the sake of his given word Titus restrained his anger and forgave them, yet did not grant them terms equal to those in which the other defectors remained.

The soldiers set fire to the silver gates, and when the silver melted and ran, the flame seized the wooden boards, and from there unexpectedly spread along the passages. The Jews saw the flame around them, and their bodies and souls grew weak, and from terror no one even tried to extinguish the fire, but they stood in one place and watched.

Afterward, greatly grieved, the Jews came to their senses, but did not come to reason, and became even more enraged against the Romans. All that day and all that night the Romans set fire to houses and covered passages.

On the next day Titus sent to extinguish the fire and to level with the ground what had been burned, and to widen the path for the army. And Titus summoned six of the chief commanders: Tiberius Alexander, and Sextus Cerealis, and Larcius Lepidus, and Titus Frigius, and Fronto Eternius, and Marcus Antonius Julianus, governor of Judea; and Titus consulted with them about the temple.

Some of these commanders said: "It is fitting according to the law of war to deal with the temple, for the rebellion among the Jews will not cease while the temple stands, to which they gather from everywhere." Others of the commanders said: "If the Jews stop and do not raise weapons in the temple, then it is fitting to preserve the temple.
