The Jews stabbed the Romans in battle; and some, who had only just climbed up and had not yet managed to raise their shields, the Jews got to first and cut down. Other Jews pushed the ladders away from the walls and threw them down.

In some places the Romans raised the imperial standards. The Jews rushed at them and made a fierce slaughter, trying to seize the standards. The Romans considered the loss of a standard a great disgrace and did not retreat, but fought until all had perished.

The Jews seized the standards and killed those who had climbed onto the walls, and boasted as if they had killed or taken captive the emperor himself. But none of the Romans died without vengeance; each first killed, and only then was killed.

Titus, seeing that his mercy toward the temple was harming the soldiers, ordered the gates set on fire. At that time Ananus of Emmaus and Archelaus, son of Magadath, defected to him in the hope of obtaining pardon from Titus, since they defected when the Jews were prevailing. Titus thought they had come with deceit, for he knew the inflexibility of the Jews, and he was already minded to kill them, and said: “You have come under compulsion, not of your own free will, and are unworthy of salvation, since you defected when you saw your fatherland ablaze.” But for the sake of his given word Titus restrained his anger and pardoned them, yet he did not grant them terms equal to those enjoyed by the other defectors.

The soldiers set fire to the silver gates, and when the silver melted and ran, the flame engulfed the boards and from there unexpectedly spread along the galleries. When the Jews saw the flame around them, their bodies and souls grew weak, and from terror no one even tried to put out the fire, but they stood in one place and watched.

Afterward, greatly grieved, the Jews recovered themselves, but did not become reasonable; instead they became even more enraged at the Romans. All that day and all night the Romans set fire to houses and covered galleries.

On the next day Titus sent men to put out the fire, to level to the ground what had been burned, and to widen the way for the army. Titus summoned six of the chief commanders: Tiberius Alexander, Sextus Cerealis, Larcius Lepidus, Titus Frigius, Fronton Eternius, and Marcus Antonius Julianus, governor of Judea; and Titus consulted with them about the temple.
