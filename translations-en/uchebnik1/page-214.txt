On the next day after the Romans had entrenched themselves, many of the rebels gathered, since they lacked food, and attacked the Roman guards who were watching Mount of Olives. This was at the first hour of the day. The Jews expected that the Romans would be resting carelessly after taking food and would be frightened by the suddenness of the attack and would be defeated. But the Romans noticed the appearance of the Jews in time and ran together quickly, and prevented the enemy from entering the camp, and made a great slaughter. And much bravery was shown on both sides, for the Romans were the strongest and most experienced warriors, while the Jews did not spare themselves and in blind fury rushed at the enemy and sought death for themselves, in order to escape the cruel torturer—hunger, which was also a commander to them. An empty stomach drove the Jews to battle, while the Romans did not wish to be disgraced and to let the Jews out as if from a net or a trap. The Jews had one hope of salvation—to break through the barriers in a rush.

One Roman horseman, named Pedanius, when the Jews turned to flight and were crowding into the ravines, rode up beside them on horseback and seized one Jewish youth who was fleeing from the enemy, thick of body and heavy with weapons. And Pedanius seized the youth by the shin, and all marveled that he could lean down from his horse at full gallop, and what strength was in his arms and body, and how swift his horse was, and how skilled a rider Pedanius himself was.

Pedanius, having seized the Jewish youth, brought him like a beautiful prize to the emperor. Titus marveled at the strength of the captor, but ordered the captive to be executed. Then Titus approached the temple and urged the soldiers to finish the siege ramp. The Jews suffered frequent defeats in the battles, which went on constantly and drew ever closer to the sanctuary.

And as parts fall away from a rotting body, so the rebels cut down and burned the structures adjoining the temple, and starting from the northern colonnades they burned everything as far as the Antonia tower, and cut down the colonnades for another 20 fathoms. Thus the Jews with their own hands began to burn the sanctuary. And two days later, on the fourteenth day of the month Panemus, that is July, the Romans also set fire to the nearby colonnades. And when the fire blazed 15 cubits from the Jews, they cut down the roofing of the colonnades and leveled them with the ground, so that there would be no structures adjoining the Antonia tower.

At that time a certain Jew, short in stature and weak in appearance, of common birth, went out onto the tomb of the high priest John, and boasted, and reviled the Romans, and challenged the bravest of them to battle, and said to the Romans: “There is no one among you who can fight me one on one.” And so this Jew humbled everyone.
