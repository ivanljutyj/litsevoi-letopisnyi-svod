And the heart of the Saracen prince was softened, and in reply he sent many wondrous gifts, and returned home in peace, marveling at Nikephoros’s reason.

Empress Irene died in exile, and Emperor Nikephoros ordered her body transferred to the monastery that she herself had built on the island of Prinkipos, since she was very pious and virtuous. She established numerous shelters for strangers and the sick, founded monasteries, lightened taxes, and bestowed countless other benefits on the poor and suffering.

In place of Patriarch Tarasios, who had departed to the Lord, the asekretis Nikephoros, secretary of the imperial chancery, was elevated to the patriarchal throne of Constantinople.

Emperor Nikephoros forced Patriarch Nikephoros to restore to the clergy the presbyter Joseph, who had married Emperor Constantine to the adulteress Theodote. Although the patriarch himself did not wish it, he had to restore Joseph, fearing the emperor’s evil disposition, lest he renew iconoclasm.

Plato the recluse, with his nephews Theodore, abbot of the Stoudios Monastery, and Joseph, bishop of Thessalonica, as well as the monks in their monasteries, ceased Eucharistic communion with the patriarch, reproaching the emperor for having ordered the restoration of Joseph, who had married Emperor Constantine contrary to church canons.

The emperor assembled a council of bishops against them. The great Theodore and his companions admonished the emperor many times, but he did not heed his words, and then Theodore foretold him an inglorious end. The emperor, enraged at Theodore, sent them all into exile.

Emperor Nikephoros himself went to war against the Bulgars living near the Ister, and having defeated their capital Pliska, he burned the citadel where the palace of the aforementioned prince was located.

The Bulgarian prince sent to Emperor Nikephoros, saying: “I beg you for peace, O emperor, for you have conquered us.” But the emperor, not satisfied with the spoil, did not wish to conclude peace. Then the enraged Bulgarian prince ordered all entrances and exits to his country blocked with wooden fortifications, gathered an army in two days, and rushing at the Greeks, utterly defeated them. Bursting into the imperial tent, he cut off Nikephoros’s head and, hanging it on a tree, left it there for many days.
