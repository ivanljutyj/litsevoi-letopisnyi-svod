Theophilus, coming to his senses, raised himself a little and exclaimed: “Woe is me, wretched one! Because of the holy icons I am being scourged.” Then the queen, taking with prayer the icon of the Most Pure Mother of God, weeping, put it to Theophilus’s lips. And at once his lips closed, and his gaze became conscious.

Having come to himself, Theophilus saw beside him the icon and, taking it, kissed it. At once his insolent lips, which had reviled the holy icons, assumed their natural position, and he fell asleep, already confessing the veneration of icons. The queen brought out the holy images from her coffers and brought them to Theophilus, so that he might be finally confirmed in the Orthodox faith.

Soon after this Theophilus departs from this temporal life and dies.

The 44th Reign of Michael and Theodora, Who Established the Veneration of the Holy Icons

After the death of Theophilus, the imperial scepter passed to his son Michael, and he ruled together with his mother Theodora for four years. Michael’s tutors were appointed to be the patrician Theoctistus and the chief magistros Manuel.

But soon, after a conflict arose between them, Manuel withdrew from the palace to his house, which was near the so-called Cistern of Aspar (a city reservoir for storing water). In his house he received monks, although he himself was of noble origin.

Theodora was faithful to Orthodoxy even while her husband was alive, honored the holy icons and venerated them. Now, both by her own will and instructed by Theoctistus, patrician and canikleios logothete (that is, keeper of the imperial inkstand), Queen Theodora ordered the patriarch Annius, an iconoclast—rather a leader of demons and sorcerers than a patriarch—to be deposed and expelled from the capital. And she sent him into confinement in one of the monasteries, to a place called Knoch. There, after some time, this lawless man pierced the eyes of the Savior Christ depicted on the icon, and of His Mother and the two archangels.

The queen, inflamed with divine zeal, ordered that he himself be blinded and subjected to two hundred blows with ox sinews.

When he died, King Michael ordered his body, like the vile body of Copronymus, to be taken from the grave and subjected to outrage—dragged through the whole city, beaten with maglavia, that is, clubs.

But Methodius, confessor of Christ, King Michael raises to the throne and installs like a lamp on a lampstand, so that he might drive away the darkness of heresy. And all the Orthodox who are in exile and in prisons he delivers from bonds and sets free.
