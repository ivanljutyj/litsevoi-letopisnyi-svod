The tormentors and brigands were still warmed by the thought of the underground passages, and in them they placed their last hope. They thought that if they hid in the underground passages, they would not be found, and that if the Romans seized the Upper City from all sides, they themselves would flee into the underground passages and remain there until the Romans withdrew, and then come out and disperse wherever each could. This was like a dream to them, for it was impossible for them to escape either from God or from the Romans. But then, as was said, they still hoped in the underground passages, and therefore they themselves burned more than the Romans did. If anyone ran from the burning buildings to the underground passages, such people were killed and plundered, and food mixed with blood was eaten. The rebels already fought among themselves for food, and I think that if they had not perished in the war, they would themselves have eaten the dead because of their great obstinacy and vileness.

The emperor saw that the Upper City could not be taken without an embankment, since the city stands above the ravines. And Titus distributed the troops for the work. This was on the 20th day of the month Loos, that is, August. It was hard to carry wood and earth, since everything around the city for 100 versts was bare. Four legions set up their embankments on the western side of the city opposite the royal palace, while the auxiliary troops made embankments on the side where the bridge and the tower of Simon Niger were, in which he, earlier, when he fought with John, that is, Johanan, had made himself a refuge.

In those days the Idumean commanders secretly gathered and consulted about leaving the rebels and surrendering to the Romans. And they sent five envoys to Titus with a request for mercy. Titus thought that this would weaken the leaders of the brigands, and they would be forced to surrender to him if the Idumeans left them, for the Idumeans were a large part of the rebels’ army. Therefore Titus promised to pardon them and reluctantly sent the envoys back.

When the Idumeans prepared to leave, Simon Niger noticed it, and immediately killed there five men who had gone as envoys to Titus, and seized the Idumean commanders and put them in prison, and set guards to watch the Idumean people.

However, although the rebels diligently guarded the walls and gates, many Jews ran over to the Romans, and they accepted everyone, since Titus, in his mildness, had softened the former harsh orders. The soldiers had had their fill of slaughter, and, in search of plunder, avoided killing.
