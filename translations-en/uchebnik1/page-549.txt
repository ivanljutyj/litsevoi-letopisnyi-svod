All the golden veins—that is, the imperial treasures—he drained on his outrages and on the buffoons who surrounded him. There too were lost the precious ornaments of former emperors, the golden trees with sweet-voiced birds, and the lions hammered from solid gold. Everything that had formerly adorned the imperial palace and delighted the eyes, which Emperor Theophilus had so zealously gathered to glorify the Greek kingdom, was scattered to the winds and senselessly squandered on his vile amusements.

And if at first the audacity of these shameless men, who lived like beasts, extended only to what surrounded them, then, behaving more unrestrainedly day by day, they finally came to blaspheme against the Holy Mysteries, before which even the Seraphim tremble. Sacrilegiously enacting the celebration of the Liturgy, they poured mustard into the chalice, making sport where there is no place for sport and mocking what must not be mocked. The mother, seeing such a life of Michael, her son and the Greek emperor, grieved in her heart and was indignant in her soul, trying by her admonitions to bring him to his senses and turn him from vile and foul deeds. But this was like speaking to the dead or singing to the deaf.

Empress Theodora sent the logothete Theoctistus on ships with a numerous army to recover for the Greeks Crete, which had been seized by the Agarenes.

The well-armed army at first terrified the Agarenes, and they could not withstand the Greeks.

Then the Agarenes spread a rumor that Theodora had handed the kingdom over to another emperor. Struck by this report, though no one was pursuing him, Theoctistus boarded a ship and hastily returned to Constantinople, abandoning his army.

But Amer, who was fighting against the Greeks, captured almost their entire army, and only a few managed to return.

Soon Michael and Theodora again sent the same logothete Theoctistus, in whom they still trusted, with a large army against the Agarenes. He fought with Amer at the Red River and returned defeated. Many were killed, while others went over to Amer's side of their own accord because of Theoctistus's severity.

Theoctistus again returned to Constantinople empty-handed. When the military operations were being discussed, Bardas, the empress's brother, laid the blame for the defeat on Theoctistus, saying: "He is guilty of the destruction of the Greek soldiers."
