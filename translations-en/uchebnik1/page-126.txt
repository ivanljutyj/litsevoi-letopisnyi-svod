Seeing this shouting and weeping, Joseph smiled and said: “The time has come, O friends, to win endless glory and to perform deeds that will be remembered by posterity!”

And Joseph gathered the best warriors, went out against the Roman guard detachments, scattered them, and cut them down as far as the place where they had entrenched themselves; he broke up their tortoise-shelters, leveled the embankment they had made with the ground, burned all their structures, and for three days and three nights did not rest, doing all this.

Vespasian, seeing that his soldiers could not withstand the raids of the Jews, ordered that they should not engage men who longed for death, for there is nothing stronger than desperate men.

And Vespasian ordered the Arab archers and Syrian stone-throwers to fight the Jews. The Jews came on, sparing not their bodies, attacked the Romans fiercely, and perished under arrows and stones.

And again Vespasian roused his soldiers as before, stationed archers, restored the embankment, and set up the “ram.” So was called a beam, very large and thick, like a ship’s mast, and at its end was made a great iron horned head; therefore this engine is called a “ram.”

And they dug in two posts and placed the “ram” between them, and wrapped it with many ropes, and pulled on them, and struck. And the archers shot, and the javelin- and stone-throwers hurled their javelins and launched stones, so that the Jews could not stand on their walls.

Therefore the Romans without fear struck the city walls with the ram. And many times they struck one place, and the walls shook. Joseph made sacks of oxhides, stuffed them, and hung them from the walls at the places where the ram’s blow was directed, and the walls suffered no harm, because the sacks hindered the ram.

The Romans tied sickles to spears and cut down the sacks.

The Jews, in despair, took bundles of splinters and sticks, dry branches, pitch, and sulfur, and went out from the city in three places, set fire to the embankment with its structures and siege engines, and the ram. The Romans were terrified by their fury and fled, and flame surrounded them, and they could not escape, and stabbed themselves.

Then one man among the Jews performed a deed worthy of remembrance. This was Eleazar, son of Samai, by birth from a village in Galilee. He lifted a very great stone and threw it upon the ram and knocked off its “head,” and leaped down from the city wall and seized that head while among the Romans, and returned and stood on the city wall, so that all might see and praise him, and so held the ram’s head.
