To Theophilus came a poor woman with a complaint against a noble and wealthy patrician named Petronius (the Greeks called him Dongar). Bitterly lamenting and weeping, she began to beg Theophilus to be her advocate. Petronius had built himself a huge house next to her little home, and its shadow had completely eclipsed the sunlight for her. Unable to endure this, she was forced to leave her dwelling.

Having heard her and learned the details of the case, the emperor became convinced of Petronius’s guilt and ordered that his house, which had entirely blocked the woman’s light, be razed to the ground, and that Petronius himself be led out to the marketplace and beaten with oxhide thongs.

But Emperor Theophilus defended justice not only in court. His heart was free also from greed. He did not wish, for example, to profit from the wine trade, as the following bears witness.

Once the emperor was standing on a height near the imperial palace, where there stood a famous statue carved from fine marble—a mighty snow-white ox, thrown down and mauled by a lion (which is why the place was called the Ox Market). The emperor surveyed the sea and saw a laden ship sailing toward the shore. A light wind was driving it on, and it was already approaching the harbor. Then the emperor asked: “Whose ship is this, and where is it from?” And when he heard that the ship together with its cargo belonged to the empress, his heart was filled with sorrow.

Summoning the empress and questioning her about the ship, he exclaimed: “O empress! Where is the imperial dignity, and what has it become? The Greek emperor has turned out to be a merchant and a wine-seller! Alas, worse than a trader!” And having said this, he ordered the fully laden ship, with all its cargo, to be burned with the flame of all-devouring fire. He restored justice to such a degree that for a long time he commanded inquiry to be made whether anyone had a case against another, and no such persons were found.

Emperor Theophilus himself devoted himself greatly to reading books. Like an industrious bee gathering nectar from the flowers of the field, he studied Holy Scripture. When he came upon a passage difficult to understand, he made notes on a wax tablet, hoping that what he could not grasp with his own mind would be explained to him. But none of those knowledgeable in Scripture, wise and learned men, succeeded in this, and the emperor grieved greatly over it.

Seeing his sorrow, one of the emperor’s chamberlains began to praise the great Methodius and told Theophilus of his wisdom, his great learning, and his God-enlightened mind (whether by God’s Providence or because he cherished in his heart love for the God-bearing man).
