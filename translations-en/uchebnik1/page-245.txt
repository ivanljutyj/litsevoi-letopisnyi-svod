And then Titus continued his journey, soon crossed the desert, and came to Alexandria, and intended to sail from there to Italy. And the two regiments that were with him Titus sent back, and from among the captives he ordered to be led to Italy Simon Niger, and John, that is, Johanan, and seven hundred chosen Jews, distinguished among others by tall stature and bodily beauty, wishing to lead them before the Roman people during the triumphal procession in Rome.

Titus sailed from Egypt to Italy, and there was no sea swell or storm. The Romans arranged a meeting for him and received him just as they had his father. Even greater honor was shown to Titus, since his father himself rode out to meet and receive him. The townspeople felt wondrous and inexpressible joy at seeing both sovereigns riding together.

After a few days all three sovereigns—the father and two sons—consulted about arranging a common triumphal procession. The city’s counselors spoke to them of three separate processions, in honor of each victor individually. The sovereigns then announced the day on which they wished to hold a joint triumphal procession. On that day none of the countless population of Rome remained in their houses, but all went out and stood wherever each could, and all places were filled with people, and they could not all fit, and they climbed on one another. All the houses, palaces, and roofs were filled. Only a place was left for the procession to pass. The soldiers with their commanders had come since night and stood at the palace gates and at the temple of Isis, for the sovereigns were then resting there.

In the morning, at sunrise, Vespasian and Titus came out, crowned with laurel branches, dressed in purple robes, and went to the building of Octavia. There too were the counselors, the most honored officials, nobles, commanders, and administrators, and all awaited the arrival of the sovereigns. Two ivory thrones were arranged for them before the covered passages, on which they sat. And when the sovereigns appeared, the soldiers cried out, glorifying, praising, and honoring their wisdom, courage, and valor, and testified to their meekness, humility, justice, and generosity. The soldiers stood without weapons, in beaver garments, crowned with laurel branches.

Vespasian accepted the soldiers’ praise and, since they continued to cry out, signaled them to be silent. Deep silence fell among the soldiers.
