And the autocrat took great care in this matter and was anxious, for the strong Commagenian city called Samosata stood on the Euphrates, and therefore it was very easy to cross over from there to the Parthians. And the autocrat sent a message to Caesennius, that he should take care of everything and do what he considered useful in this matter. Caesennius, having received such an order, did not delay.

Suddenly, when Antiochus suspected nothing, Caesennius rode into the land of Commagene and took command of the sixth legion. And much cavalry and infantry joined him, and the kings: Aristobulus of Chalcis and Soemus of Emesa. These forces without difficulty and without resistance occupied Commagene. Of the local inhabitants no one wished to oppose them.

Antiochus had not even thought of warring with the Romans, and decided to leave his kingdom and depart with his wife and children, and thus to appear innocent before the Romans, and he withdrew from the city 120 versts and stopped there.

Caesennius sent soldiers and took Samosata. He himself with the remaining soldiers pursued Antiochus.

Antiochus did not wish to offer any armed resistance, but, lamenting his fate, submitted and wished rather to accept any misfortune than to take up arms. But the young men, immature and inexperienced, hoping in their own strength, advised Antiochus's sons, Epiphanes and Callinicus, not to submit as their father had, but to repel the attackers. And then Antiochus's sons decided to take up arms. And a fierce battle took place that day, and no one won, and toward evening they parted. Antiochus, although his soldiers were not defeated, did not wish to resist the Romans, and, taking his wife and daughter, went to Cilicia. By this action Antiochus deprived his soldiers of courage, for they saw that he himself had acknowledged himself guilty and had renounced the kingdom. Then Antiochus's soldiers went over to the Romans.

Epiphanes with his brother gathered ten horsemen around themselves and crossed the Euphrates, and then without fear came to Vologeses, king of Parthia. And Vologeses received them not as fugitives, but honored them as kings, in memory of their former glory, and of their royal dignity, and of their wealth.

Antiochus, on the way to Tarsus, was seized by a centurion sent by Caesennius and sent to Rome in iron chains. Vespasian did not wish to receive him as a prisoner, but he remembered their former friendship, and did not hold anger against Antiochus for the guilt of the revolt, and, when Antiochus was still far from Rome, Vespasian ordered the chains to be cut and him to be released.
