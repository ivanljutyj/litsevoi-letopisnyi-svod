And all the accounts informed the emperor about Helen, and how the image and likeness of him born from her was revealed in the Serpny place — an infant shining with a royal appearance. Hearing this, Consta was at first amazed at what the elders told him, and then, understandably, began to question them about Helen, wishing to see her. He also remembered the vision he had seen with Helen: a dark night illuminated by sunbeams.

Understanding that the vision had come true and learning of its fulfillment, he received the messengers worthily and, honoring them with gifts, sent them with diverse instructions to the Persian land, with a carriage and many wagons, with provision for many comforts and with gifts sent by the emperor, to bring Helen with the youth Constantine.

They, having soon arrived, took Helen with the youth and seated her in the imperial carriage, and brought them to Emperor Consta. When he saw them, Consta rejoiced greatly over both, embraced the youth and kissed him, and loved the young woman also, and brought them inside the royal palace.

Instructed by his father in the holy books, the most wise Saint Constantine possessed all things; from his youth he had a royal mind and piety, and the wise virtue of manliness.

Rumor of the remarkable event concerning Helen spread, and this report reached Consta’s lawful wife, named Theodora, the own daughter of Herculius. By her he had three sons and a daughter, whose names were Constantius, Anvalian, Dalmatius, and the daughter Constantia, wife of Licinius. Yet none of them was worthy of their father’s kingdom.

Consta also feared lest out of jealousy the young woman with her son be killed by treachery, and prudently sent the youth with letters to Emperor Diocletian and to his son-in-law Maximian, who then lived in Bithynia, in the city of Nicomedia. Having received the emperor’s son at the royal court, they assigned to him a detachment of imperial guards, since he was a royal offspring, that is, a pledge of peace, and he grew up in the palace.

Consta feared the like also concerning Helen, lest his wife Theodora devise something against her, as he had already said before. When the youth Constantine grew up, he was taught Hellenic learning. But the demon, hating the good, stirred up envious men against him — idolaters from among the sorcerers and soothsayers living under Diocletian and Maximian Herculius. And foreseeing that he would become not only emperor but also a pious king and a destroyer and ruiner of the Hellenic heresy, and also a great preacher of God’s kingdom of Christ, for this reason they devised manifold and all kinds of intrigues against him in order to kill him.
