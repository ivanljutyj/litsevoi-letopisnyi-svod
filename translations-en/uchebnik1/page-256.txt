It never rotted. Wine, oil, dates, and other produce always remained fresh and whole. Many years had passed since the city was founded, and more than seventy since Herod had built it up.

And when the Romans took the fortress, they found all the fruits that Herod had stored up in fear of war still intact. This came about because of the favorable composition of the air, for owing to the height of the place the air there was free of earthly impurities. There was also discovered there the weaponry stored by Herod: twenty thousand suits of armor, as many shields, and other weapons in the same quantity, and countless quantities of plain iron, copper, and tin. Herod prepared all this for himself, since he feared two things: first, that the Jews might overthrow him and restore the former kings whom he had driven out; the second and even greater danger came from Cleopatra, queen of Egypt, who many times urged Antony to kill Herod and give her the kingdom of Judea. Therefore it is worthy of wonder that Antony, enslaved by his lust for Cleopatra and doing everything she commanded, in this one matter did not obey her. It was because of these fears that Herod fortified Masada as has been described.

Silva, the Roman commander, enclosed the entire fortress and found one place where an embankment could be built. At the base of the cliff on which Masada stood, on the western side, there was a broad valley. The fortress rose 300 cubits above this valley. At the base of this cliff Silva ordered the soldiers to build the embankment. They worked briskly with all their strength and made the embankment 200 cubits high. But the earth could not hold above that height, so they began to lay stones and raised the embankment another 50 cubits.

Then battering engines, stone-throwing engines, and spear-throwing engines were set up, with which the Romans prevented the enemy from appearing on the walls of the fortress. The rams struck repeatedly and destroyed part of the wall. The Sicarii built an inner wall in this place, not of stone but wooden on both sides and filled with earth in the middle. When the ram struck, it only packed the newly heaped earth more tightly.

Silva despaired of taking the wall with rams and resolved to burn it. When the wall blazed up in flame, a terrible storm arose from the north and turned the fire against the Romans, who fled from the flames. The Roman embankments burned, and the battering engines as well. The Jews shouted with joy, as if they had won.
