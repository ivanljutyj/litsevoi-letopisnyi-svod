And to the petitioner Apsimar he promised to sell the mutilated Justinian for gold. O gold, most insolent traitor! O gold, seller of friends and murderer of kin! How you accomplish even what cannot be accomplished, and force nature and kinship to be wronged. But what must come to pass by God’s judgments, by God’s Providence, neither an iron wall nor fiery force will hold back. For Justinian learned of the Khagan’s deceit and conspiracy, and fled from there, and attached to himself—that is, won over—Tervel, prince of the Bulgars, with many promises.

Then Justinian came into the Byzantine land, to the reigning city itself, and brought many legions of Tervel’s army. With a few of them Justinian took counsel, and gained their trust, and with them found the water-bearing sulinarium, that is, the aqueduct, in order to cut off the water conducted into the city, for Justinian knew this from former times. And because of thirst for water the city was delivered to Justinian.

The 29th Reign, of Justinian the Noseless, that is, with his nose cut off, who reigned in Constantine’s city

After Apsimar, Justinian the Noseless received the kingdom a second time, and remained so for 7 years, during which he did many evils, which this narrative will set forth. To the Bulgar prince Justinian, fulfilling what he had promised, gave richly abundant gifts.

In Constantine’s city Justinian consigned many of the boyar families to fire, and plundered their possessions and herds.

From among the great men Justinian slaughtered many; Leontius he killed together with Apsimar, as robbers and thieves—that is, plunderers—with his heavy scepter. Thus the wicked are subject to judgment: even if they escape torment at first, afterward they receive worse torment. Leontius and Apsimar received a twofold—that is, double—vengeance: not only did they themselves suffer, but they were also responsible for the misfortune of many others. Justinian’s avenging sword visited torments not only on ordinary people but also on the boyars, because he himself had suffered at their hands.

Kallinikos, who had been patriarch in Constantinople, Justinian tortured and mocked. He also subjected his young and foolish children to insult. Finally Justinian extinguished the patriarch Kallinikos’s bodily lights of the heart—his eyes—and sent him into exile in Rome.

And in place of Kallinikos Justinian appointed as patriarch Kyros the Recluse, who had been in the monastery of Amastris, since he had foretold to him: “You will reign a second time.”

Justinian became exceedingly angry with all, and as if he had gone mad, and he killed countless people from the townspeople and from the army; some he openly beheaded, while others, sewn into sacks at night, he threw into the sea.
