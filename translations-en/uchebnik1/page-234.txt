In the evening the soldiers stopped the slaughter, and at night the fire continued just the same. In the morning, on the eighth day of the month Gorpiaeus, that is, September, the sun shone forth, and Jerusalem could be seen blazing in the fire. This city had endured so much calamity and want that, if it had received as much good from the beginning of its creation, all lands would have envied it.

Titus entered the city and marveled at its strength, at the solidity of the walls and towers, which the rebels in their madness had left. And Titus examined the height of these structures, and the size of the stones of which they were made, the soundness of the joining of these stones, and their width and length, and Titus said to his soldiers: “God helped us fight, and God is the destroyer of these Jewish strongholds, for human hands and cunning and siege engines could not have overcome these towers.” Then Titus spoke much in this vein to his commanders. And Titus released the captives and the fettered from the prisons.

And afterward Titus razed the whole city to the ground and destroyed the walls. Only those three towers he left as a memorial of his good fortune and courage, so that later generations might see and praise him, since he had conquered what could not be conquered by natural strength.

The soldiers, however, grew weary of killing, and seeing that much people still remained, they asked the emperor to kill only those who resisted and to spare the rest. And Titus commanded: “Do so.” Some of them, however, violated this order and killed the old and infirm. The young and fit they drove into the temple court, to the place where the women stood during the services. Fronto was assigned to guard this crowd of captives, so that he might judge what fate each of them deserved and separate those who were going to execution from those who were to be saved. Youths with the longest necks and the most beautiful were chosen to be kept for the victory procession, that is, the triumph.

The rebels and robbers themselves betrayed one another and were executed. The rest, those older than seventeen, were sent in chains to labor in Egypt; and many Titus sent as gifts to foreign lands, so that they might perish in the spectacles by beasts and by iron. And those who were under seventeen, Titus sold them all. While Fronto was sorting the captives, eleven thousand of them died of hunger. Some did not want to touch Roman food, because they loathed it. Others would gladly have eaten if it had been given to them, but the guards hated them and gave them no food, since wheat had become scarce because of the multitude of people.
