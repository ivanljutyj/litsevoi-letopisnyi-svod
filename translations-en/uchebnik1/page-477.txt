With a small retinue he entered, and sailed by night, and came to Constantinople.

In the morning, clad in royal garments, he entered the royal vessel, that is, the ship, and joined battle with the enemy; and they, surrounding him with ships, fought fiercely, thinking that he was the emperor. But he was brave and killed many, and was himself wounded by them, that is, injured, and died, fulfilling what is written: “Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his soul for his friends.”

But the emperor Constans, grandson of Heraclius, kept the root of the cunning, from the beginning impious heresy of those who taught that in Christ there is one will; and then the emperor brought forth bitter fruits, of which many ate, and from these we too have learned what sharpness and bitterness those fruits have. But Maximus, the goodly-victorious—the golden-forged trumpet, the gold-flowing Nile of Orthodoxy, the reprover of such cunning heresy—partook of this bitterness to the last fullness. His right hand was cut off by the command of the impious Constans with the sword, and his honey-flowing tongue—alas for me!—was cut out: those parts that poured forth streams of divine things, for Maximus wrote and spoke much in defense of Orthodoxy. And again God restored his hand and tongue to him. Constans did the same also to two Anastasii, disciples of this Maximus. He also condemned the great Martin, bishop of old Rome—whom the whole Greek people called pope—to exile in Cherson.

Moreover, Constans also became like Cain through brother-murder, killing Theodosius, his brother, who sprang, that is, grew, from the same field and seed as he. But many of the Orthodox who did not wish to accept his one-will heresy the emperor ordered to be wounded and beaten, and led naked through the midst of the city, and mocked, and he condemned them to exile. But the emperor himself, having done so much evil, sailed to Sicily, desiring to take away the beauty from the greatly gifted imperial city and to grant the empire to old Rome, as if someone should strip the radiant bride of her adornment and adorn some ancient old woman. But the plans of men are vain if God does not speed them, that is, does not help.

Constans also sent for his three sons—Constantine, Heraclius, and Tiberius—to Constantinople; but the city’s people did not give them up to him. Constans remained in Sicily for six years, and there, by his deserts, he drank the bitter cup of death. When he was washing in warm waters, one of those serving him struck him on the head with a basin, and so Constans died.

And Mezezius was named emperor, a man from Sicily, with differently colored eyes and a handsome face. Yet he did not long enjoy power, but quickly withered and vanished without appearance—like a rosebud, that is, a rose, which soon falls when it appears.
