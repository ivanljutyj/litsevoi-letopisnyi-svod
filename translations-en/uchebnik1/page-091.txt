Varus with an army went against Caesar’s subjects who had stirred up war, and fought with many, and many Romans and Jews perished.

And then the Jews submitted to Varus and agreed to hand over the ringleaders. And Varus sent for them, and they were brought. He put the elders in prison, and of the younger men he crucified 2,000. It was reported to him that in Idumea there were ten thousand brigands, and Varus went against them. When they learned of this, they sent Achiab, the cousin of Archelaus, to Varus, and submitted to him. Varus pardoned the people, but sent the leaders to Caesar for determination of guilt. Among the leaders were also relatives of Archelaus, and Caesar ordered them to be tortured, since they had made war against the king who was their relative. Varus by his own forces pacified Jerusalem and left a guard in it, which had been there before, and he himself returned to Antioch.

Against Archelaus there was yet another revolt; the Jews in Rome rose up against him. Fifty of the most influential men came from Judea to Caesar and asked that he give them another ruler, and they slandered their own king. And the Jews living in Rome supported them, of whom there were 8,000. Caesar gathered the influential Romans and representatives of all who were under his authority in the temple of Apollo, which was in his palace; he himself had built this temple and wonderfully arranged and adorned everything there.

Then on one side stood the influential Jews with the people, and opposite them stood Archelaus with his supporters. But Archelaus’s relatives despised him and envied him, and did not stand on his side; yet they did not join Archelaus’s opponents either, since they respected Caesar and feared him; and therefore they stood apart. Philip also was there, the brother of Archelaus, who pursued a double aim. On the one hand, he had come to support Archelaus and receive honor from him; on the other hand, in case Caesar should divide Herod’s kingdom among all his relatives, Philip wanted to receive his share. Caesar ordered Herod’s opponents to tell what they accused Herod of. And they said: “This Herod was not a king to us, but a tormentor such as there had never been. He killed many, and we, the living, suffered such evil from him that we envied those who died. Not only did he torment the bodies of his subjects, but he also ruined the cities subject to him. Yet he adorned the cities of foreigners and gave Jewish blood over to strangers. We were prosperous and observed the ancestral law, but instead of this, under Herod, extreme poverty and lawlessness seized our people. Truly, even when our forefathers returned from Babylon, in the reign of Xerxes, there was no such calamity. Over the long years of the Babylonian captivity our people did not endure as much grief as in the short time of Herod’s reign.
