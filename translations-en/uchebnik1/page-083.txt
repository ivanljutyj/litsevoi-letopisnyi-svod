The loathsome serpent was sated with my benefactions, and then turned his greed against me as well. By my living I vexed him, and my old age was a burden to him. He could not endure to receive the kingdom lawfully, but truly plotted to kill me, his father. I brought him forward, though he was rejected, and I neglected the sons born to me by the queen, and appointed this one my heir.

"I acknowledge before you, Varus, my folly, for I myself turned those sons against me when I took from them their lawful hopes, and all for the sake of this Antipater. I did not show them as many benefactions as I showed to this same Antipater. While still alive I nearly yielded my kingdom to him, and wrote in my will that he should reign after me, and from the tribute I collected I assigned him 100 talents of gold, and from my palace I bestowed many gifts on him. When he traveled to Rome I gave him 300 talents of gold, and I vouched for him before Caesar, and called him my savior, and singled him out above all my family. But what dishonor did those sons of mine commit comparable to what Antipater did? And what witnesses stood against them comparable to those who accuse Antipater? And what does the parricide dare to say? Does he hope to cover the truth with falsehood? You, Varus, beware of him. I know this serpent well and foresee all his flattering speech and his feigned tears. It was he who told me before to beware of Alexander and advised that not everyone should come near my person. It was he who escorted me to my bed, and gave me a carefree life, and guarded my sleep—my comforter, my defender, and my bodyguard. When I think, O Varus, of his lies and hypocrisy, I do not understand how I am still alive, and I marvel how I escaped the snares of such a dreadful villain. Since God in His anger is thus destroying my house, and my favorite rises against me, I with groaning bring the unjust man to judgment. And no one who seeks my blood will escape punishment, even if all my children prove guilty."

Having said this, the king could not speak for sobs and tears, and he ordered Nicolaus, his nobleman, to bring the proofs and report Antipater's crimes. Then Antipater raised his head, while still lying at his father's feet, and said: "You yourself, father, have justified me. How can I be a parricide when you yourself say that I was always your guardian? How can my good deeds be called hypocrisy? How can I be called a cunning serpent if I understand neither the past nor the future? Or how was I so wise in other matters and in this great matter proved so foolish? Or how, with my intelligence, did I not realize that, having committed such baseness, I could hide neither from men nor, all the more, from the Heavenly Judgment, since God sees all things in heaven, on earth, and in the sea—everywhere?
