If we do not first report such an outrage to Florus, that chief of evil, he will accuse us of disorder.

Agrippa, however, thought it wrong to write to the emperor against Florus, yet he also considered it disadvantageous to himself to disregard the Jews. So Agrippa summoned the people to the palace of the Hasmoneans, which was on the other side of the upper city, and a marble bridge had been built from the palace to the temple. And Agrippa delivered this speech before the people: "If I saw that you were all eager to make war on the Romans, and if I did not understand that there are among you the most honored and best men, who think of peace, I would not address you or try to bring you to your senses. For when all who hear have unanimously resolved on evil, then every word and every instruction in righteousness will be vain and superfluous. But I see that some of you are driven to war by your age, since you have not yet experienced what war is; others hope without grounds to gain freedom by war; and others burn with desire to plunder and oppress the weak when everything falls into confusion. Seeing all this, I have resolved to incline you to prudence, so that those who wish for good may not suffer, and I hope that my speech will be useful to you all. Do not interrupt me, even if you hear unpleasant words. For those who have irrevocably resolved on war will be able to hold the same thought of war even after my words. But if all do not calm down, my words will not reach those who want to listen. I know that many speak of the injustices they suffer from Roman governors, and at the same time praise freedom. But if you are angry only at the governors who oppress you, why do you praise freedom? If slavery seems unacceptable to you, there is no point in reviling the governors, since even if they ruled mildly, slavery itself would still be shameful. Consider how small the occasion for war is, for the offenses of the governors are not so very great. And one should please rulers, not provoke them. If a small occasion provokes great abuse from you, then by this you will turn even more against yourselves those whom you abuse. Before, they harmed you not openly but secretly, with fear; but now, seeing your disobedience and disrespect, they will destroy and plunder you openly. Even if Roman officials are cruel, not all Romans oppress you, nor is the emperor, against whom you have gathered to fight. For governors are not sent to us from Rome with orders from the emperor to be unjust. Those who are in the western lands cannot see or quickly learn what is done in the eastern lands. And it is not fitting, because of enmity with one person, to fight against many, and because of a small thing to rise against the great.
