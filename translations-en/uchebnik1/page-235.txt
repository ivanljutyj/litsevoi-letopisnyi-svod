War came. The stuffiness and crowding led them first to plague, and then to swift famine.

If anyone says that such a multitude of people could not have been accommodated in that city, let him learn of Cestius’s count. When Nero set the Jewish people at nothing and expected no resistance from them, Cestius, to show him the might of Jerusalem, asked the high priests to count the people as best they could.

And the feast of Passover came, on which it is the custom to bring sacrifice from the ninth hour to the eleventh. And more than ten people gathered for each sacrifice, since a sacrifice could not be eaten by one person alone. Sometimes even twenty people gathered for one sacrifice. And the priests counted 255,600 sacrifices. If one counts only ten people for each sacrifice, and almost disregards the larger number, it comes to 2,900,000 people. Moreover these were only pure, undefiled, sanctified people. Others—those not sanctified, or defiled in any way, or leprous, or sinful, or foreigners, or women in impurity—all such were not allowed to touch the sacrifice. And then, before the war, as has already been said, by God’s Providence the whole Jewish people came into the city as into a prison; and war suddenly encircled them. The multitude of the dead surpassed all that could have been expected from Higher Providence or from men.

The Romans dug out the underground passages and found those hiding there, and killed all whom they found. In the underground passages 4,000 dead were also found. Some cut their own throats, some killed one another among their own people, and others died of hunger. A terrible stench coming from the corpses met those who entered the underground passages, because of which many ran out holding their noses. Others, enslaved by the passion of love of money, rummaged among the corpses in search of wealth and found many costly things.

And all foul paths were not considered such if only there was hope of gain.

God also repaid the two chief tormentors according to their deserts. John, that is Jochanan, was starving and was underground with his brother. Earlier he had been very proud and had refused the mercy the Romans offered him; now he himself asked the Romans for mercy. Simon Niger was very strongly pressed, about which we shall tell later, and he himself surrendered to the Romans. And the Romans kept Simon Niger for execution during the triumph, that is the victorious procession. John, that is Jochanan, was condemned to lifelong imprisonment.
