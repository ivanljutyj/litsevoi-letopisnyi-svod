And the Jews by their cunning scattered their embankments, and the wall-breaking engines could not overcome the firmness of the walls. When they met in battle, the rebellious spirit of the Jews prevailed.

And the Jewish stronghold proved stronger than famine, and internal strife, and war. The onslaughts of the Jews were invincible, and their cheerfulness amid disasters was beyond telling.

Therefore the Romans guarded the siege embankments firmly. John, that is Johanan, with his associates in the Tower of Antonia, understood that he would not be saved if the Romans breached the walls. When the Romans were setting up the “ram,” the Jews attacked them but achieved nothing. They came with bundles of splinters, but fled before they came near. The Jews were lukewarm and spiritless. And not all together, but little by little they came out, and acted out of concert, and slowly, and cautiously, and fearfully—in short, as was uncharacteristic of Jews.

The Jews came out indecisively, but the Romans arrayed themselves firmly and blocked every approach to the embankments with their bodies and weapons, so that there was no possibility of setting them on fire. And each of the Romans was strengthened in spirit and resolved not to leave the battle until death. For all hope of taking the city would be cut off from the Romans if these structures of theirs also were burned. They would also have been greatly ashamed if they were again overcome: the weak by the mighty, the despairing by warriors, the inexperienced by the experienced, the Jews by the Romans. Therefore the Jews, frightened, galloped back, reproaching one another for cowardice. This was on the first day of the month Panemus, that is, July. When the Jews retreated, the Romans brought up engines for taking the city. Meanwhile the Jews struck them from the Tower of Antonia with stones, and fire, and iron, and by every possible means. Although the Jews hoped in the strength of the walls and did not fear the “rams,” they still fought the Romans from the height, hindering the setting up of the engines. The Romans, however, thought that the zeal of the Jews came from the weakness of the Tower of Antonia and struck the walls even harder. Often striking, the Roman soldiers did not yield before what flew at them from above, yet they only slightly damaged the walls. Other Romans covered themselves with shields and began to dig with their hands and iron tools, and with difficulty broke four stones out of the wall and dragged them away from the great mountain.

At night calm came, and all rested. The walls, however, suddenly fell, having been shaken all day by the “rams.” That section of the wall collapsed where John, that is Johanan, had earlier made a mine to get under the Roman embankment. After this event both sides acted wondrously. The Jews, who ought to have grieved over the destruction of the wall, contrary to expectation conceived great hope, for the Tower of Antonia stood nearby.
