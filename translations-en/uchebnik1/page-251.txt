The Jews who were in the Lower Fortress, having learned of these negotiations, decided to flee secretly by night. Those in the Upper Fortress learned of the impending escape and sent to Bassus and told him about it. Partly those in the Upper Fortress envied those below, because they alone would be saved from the Romans, and partly they feared the commander, lest he lay the blame for this escape on them too.

The bravest of the Jews broke through the Roman army and fled, while those who remained inside, numbering 1,700, were killed, and their wives and children were taken captive.

Bassus kept the Jews of the Upper Fortress, who had fulfilled their promises, unharmed and returned Eleazar to them. And then Bassus rode off, having readied his soldiers for battle, into the forest called Iardes, for he had been informed that in that place many Jews had gathered who had fled from the captured fortresses and from Jerusalem during the past war.

When he came to the forest, Bassus surrounded it with horsemen so that no one could escape, and he placed foot soldiers to cut down the trees. The Jews were in the hands of the Romans, but they did not submit and decided it was better to accomplish a glorious feat and perish than to submit and remain alive. And the Jews showed their nature, desiring either to perish or to break through and flee.

Together, with a loud shout, the Jews rushed and attacked the Romans who surrounded them. The latter stood against them firmly. Despair led the Jews, and fear of disgrace led the Romans. The losses were unequal: on the Roman side 12 men fell and a few were wounded, while of the Jews no one escaped, but all fell, numbering 30,000, including their commander Judas, son of Jair.

At the same time the emperor sent a message to Bassus and to Maximus that they should give the whole land of Judea to the Jews; to his own soldiers he gave only one city, called Emmaus; and he imposed tribute on the Jews: to bring two zolotniks per person each year to the Capitol, as formerly to the Jerusalem temple. And so life in Judea was arranged.

Vespasian was in the fourth year of his reign when it happened that Antiochus, king of Commagene, fell into great trouble for the following reason. The Syrian governor Caesennius Paetus sent a letter to the emperor with these words: “Antiochus with his son have conspired to rebel against you. They have found for themselves a helper and like-minded ally in the person of the Parthian king.
