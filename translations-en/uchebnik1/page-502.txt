The foul Constantine ordered the body of the good-victorious martyr Euphemia to be mingled with beef and dog bones and given over to the fiery flame of the “lightning arrow.”

Hearing of this, some of the pious secretly took it and placed it in a tin reliquary, and, sealing it firmly, buried the body of the good-victorious martyr Euphemia in the sea. But in place of Euphemia’s body these pious Christians laid the body of a simple woman, which the lawless servants of Constantine, thinking it was the holy martyr’s body, by command of the impious emperor, mixed with beef and dog bones and burned.

And one could see in all cities and lands wailing upon wailing, and alas upon alas among those who lived piously, while by the impious the holy things were trampled down, and sacred vessels were reforged, and divine icons were trodden underfoot.

And many boyars and soldiers, imprisoned, who venerated icons, that accursed enemy of truth handed over to various and cruel tortures.

On the War of Artavazd against Constantine Copronymus, that is, the Dung-named

Artavazd also looked upon such events—the one of whom we said earlier in our narrative that he was joined in marriage to the daughter of Emperor Leo, the sister of this Constantine the Dung-named. This Artavazd hated the savage barbarity of the evil tormentor and made war against him, and showed him fleeing, that is, put him to flight.

And that commander Artavazd himself appeared as lord of the Greek land and as emperor, who caused the rays of piety to shine forth again, and again the dawn of gracious light shone, for the venerable images of Christ, of His Mother who gave birth to Him, and of the holy martyrs were depicted and shone forth.

But—O woe!—the crafty one again demanded that the new chosen Israel be ground like wheat, as though there were not yet enough martyrs. And for this reason, by God’s judgments, the fierce-hearted tormentor Constantine was again permitted to make war and to torment that Artavazd, as Chusarsafem the Syrian, chief of the satraps, tormented ancient Israel. What has now befallen—woe is me!—for our sins, what word can set forth? The fierce serpent Constantine Copronymus did not endure the falling away from his power; he did not lose heart, but strove, and joined himself to the generals, and approached them with flattery, and gave them hope of great gifts, and inflamed them, and drew to himself the local princes and commanders.

And he gathered a grievous force, and formed a strong warlike host, and the evil one conquers the good. Constantine overcame Artavazd and conquered.
