Jovian also wrote to the Great Athanasius, asking him to write to him concerning the true faith; Athanasius did so and sent what he had written to Jovian.

After a little time Jovian turned back toward Constantinople with his soldiers quickly because of winter, for it was severe. And when he came near the land of Galatia, Jovian died a natural death in a village called Dadastana, in Cilicia, and left great lamentation for himself among the people for six years.

The Fifth Reign: Valentinian and Valens

After Jovian, Valentinian the Great reigned for 12 years, proclaimed emperor by the soldiers at Nicaea. He had been expelled from the army by Julian because he confessed the pious faith. But God repaid him a hundredfold already in his lifetime—instead of an army, He granted him a kingdom.

This Valentinian summoned his brother Valens from a Pannonian city and set him as emperor in Constantinople and over all the East, while he took for himself the Western lands: Rome, Britain, and the others.

And then the pious Valentinian departed for Old Rome, while Valens gathered all the Arians and walked as a heretic in the path of his fathers, and sent bishops into confinement.

Valens ordered priests, deacons, and monks to be expelled from their cities, and from Alexandria, which Tatian then held. He even brought some Orthodox to tortures and delivered them to fire. Under Valens were martyred Nicetas, Amphilochius, bishop of Iconium, and two priests, Baphusius and Verk, with two daughters and two sons, and the monk Arpila, and twelve common men: Aviv, Constans, Agnus, Reas, Igathrax, Iskoi, Silas, Sigitz, Soniril, Suimvl, Ferm, Phill; and seven women: Anna, Alla, Larissa, Moiko, Mamika, Uirko, Animaida. Also martyred under Valens were Sabbas the Goth, Isaac the priest, Urban, and with him 80 priests.

Basil of Caesarea departed this life in the fifth year of Valens’s reign. And Valens did many vile, cruel, and blasphemous things against God’s Church, and he did all this after the death of Athanasius. But before that, as if restrained by some virtue, although he still threatened others, he did not dare to sin by any wicked deed against Athanasius.

But the Orthodox Christians who were in Constantinople thought to receive mercy from Valens and sent envoys to him in a friendly manner when he was in Nicomedia. They sent 80 men of clerical rank, of whom the names of three are given: Theodore, Urban, and Medimnus.
