There are three schools of teachers of the Law in Judea: the first are called Pharisees, the second Sadducees, and the third Essenes. The Essenes keep the Law in greater purity than the Pharisees and Sadducees. The Essenes reject all pleasures and approve abstinence and a life without passions. The Essenes avoid marriage and intercourse with women, but receive other people's children in early childhood, raise them in their doctrine, care for them, count them as kin, and teach them from their books. They do not reject marriage and childbearing, but they themselves abstain from relations with women, and they say that no woman can keep her husband's bed in purity. They do not desire wealth, and they have no property at all, but everything is common among them, both clothing and dwellings. They consider oil an abomination. Their garments are only white. They appoint as their overseers those whom they know well to be the best and wisest.

The Essenes do not live in one city but in all cities. Wherever anyone wishes, he forms a community, arranges a house, and lives there. If strangers and unknown people come from other cities, who also live in the same way and observe the same rules, they receive them as their own, give them drink and food. Therefore they do not take food on the road. They take only a weapon for protection from robbers. In silence, humility, and appearance they are like children who fear their teacher. They do not change clothing or footwear until these are completely worn out.

There is no trade at all among the Essenes, but each takes what he needs as his own, and no one forbids him. In standing before the Deity and in piety they surpass all. They sleep little. At night they rise for worship, glorify God, and pray to Him. Until sunrise they say nothing, but send up the ancestral prayers to the sun, as if praying for its rising.

After this their overseers dismiss them, so that each may practice the craft he has learned. And until the fifth hour they work at full strength.

And again they gather in one place, gird themselves with loincloths, and wash their bodies with cold water. Having purified themselves, they gather in another building, where none of the unbelievers is admitted.

Pure, they enter the dining hall as into a holy temple and sit there in silence; the baker distributes bread to them in order, and the cook serves dishes with a single dish. The priest prays over the food, for it is forbidden to eat without prayer. Likewise after the meal they stand and glorify God as the giver of food.
