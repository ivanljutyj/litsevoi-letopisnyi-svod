The soldiers, as usual, armed themselves and advanced like a wall, and led their horses adorned with gold and silver. And all the places before the city shone with gilded weapons. The Romans themselves looked upon themselves, and never had they a more joyful sight, nor their enemies a more terrible one. All peered out from the palaces, from the walls, and from the temple, and watched. And great fear fell upon the rebels when they saw this assembly of the whole Roman army, the beauty of the armament, the valor of the men, and the precise order. I think that at this sight they would have repented, had not despair prevented them: they no longer hoped to obtain forgiveness from the Romans for the evil they had done to the people. They knew that death in torments awaited them if they were taken alive. Therefore they chose death in battle. And it was fated that the innocent should perish with the guilty, and the city and the sanctuary, according to the prophecy of Daniel, with whom the Deity plainly conversed because of his purity. And after four days the Roman regiments finished the payment of the silver.

On the fifth day, since no offer of peace had come from the Jews, Titus divided the soldiers in two and ordered embankments to be made toward the Tower of Antonia and toward the tomb of John.

At the tomb of John the Idumeans and the soldiers of Simon Niger fought, and, running out ahead, engaged the Romans. Against those stationed at the Tower of Antonia fought the soldiers of John, that is Johanan, and the Zealots. The rebels prevailed, since they fought from above and had learned to hurl missiles with war engines. Practicing this every day, they learned little by little. They had 300 spear-throwing engines and 40 stone-throwing engines. Titus understood that the destruction and salvation of the city depended on him. Therefore he pursued its capture with all zeal, and ceaselessly sent envoys to the Jews, asking whether they would repent and surrender the city, already taken, and save the remaining part. And Titus sent Josephus to speak with the Jews in their native language.

The emperor hoped that the Jews would listen to their kinsman. This Josephus stood in a high place, beyond the reach of arrow or stone, and so that all might hear him, said: "Men of the Hebrews! Why do you not spare yourselves and the remnant of your people? Why do you bring fire upon your fatherland and upon the holy temple, upon which even foreigners look with pity and fear? They, though not sharing in the sanctuary, have a conscience and to this day have not raised their hands against it. But you, born at the sanctuary and raised there, why do you strive to destroy it? Have you not seen how walls stronger than these were destroyed? Do you not know the invincible Roman power and your slavery, which did not begin now but has passed from our forefathers to us? Consider: if anyone desires freedom, he may still renounce those masters who are weaker than you, but not those who are stronger than you."
