There was much talk on this matter—some favored John, archimandrite of Petrovsky in Moscow, and others Pimen of Pereyaslavl from Goritsy. When the boyars began to advocate for Pimen, archimandrite of Pereyaslavl, Ivan became indignant at this and said to them: “I will tell you without hesitation that you are acting wrongly before God and the Grand Prince!” From that time they began seeking an opportunity to destroy him and began to consult among themselves about it. Learning of this, he said to them: “Are you acting sincerely, not fearing to commit such evil?” They grew angry at him, seized him and bound him in iron fetters, began to starve him, and even wanted to drown him.

Such evil happened to John, archimandrite of Petrovsky, a noble and virtuous man, because he held a different opinion from Pimen and the boyars. Then they decided not to put John to death, and gradually the boyars’ malice against John, archimandrite of Petrovsky in Moscow, subsided.

Pimen, together with the boyars, examined Mityai’s sacristy and treasury and found there a charter with no text, but sealed with the Grand Prince’s seal. And after consulting with his advisers, he wrote on that charter the following: “From the tsar and Grand Prince of Rus to the tsar and patriarch. I send you Pimen; appoint him metropolitan for me; him alone I have chosen for Rus, and I have found no other better than him.”

This charter was read before the whole sacred council and the tsar. The tsar and patriarch replied to Rus, saying: “Why does the Russian prince write thus about Pimen? In Rus there should be Metropolitan Cyprian, whom the ecumenical patriarch, the most reverend Philotheus, appointed long ago. Him we send to the Russian metropolis; besides him no one can be appointed.” The Russians, on the basis of that charter, borrowed silver at interest from the Fryags and Bessermen in the name of the Grand Prince, and to this day this debt grows, already more than 20,000 rubles in silver, and after promising many rewards to many and distributing them right and left—while the gifts and presents no one can even count—they barely managed to satisfy everyone.

Then the tsar and patriarch began to question Pimen and those with him, and after many examinations they agreed to appoint Pimen metropolitan of Rus, saying: “O Russian people, whether you speak truth or falsehood, we speak the truth, act and accomplish according to truth, and we believe you.” And then the most reverend Nilus, patriarch of Constantinople, appointed Pimen metropolitan of all Rus. News of this reached Grand Prince Dmitry Ivanovich: “Your Mityai died soon near Constantinople, and Pimen has become metropolitan.”
