He labored with his righteous labors. And he entrusted this work to no one, although many of the brethren wanted to bake prosphora for him. But the venerable one did everything himself: he himself boiled kutia, made candles, and composed canons. Although he had accepted the abbacy and the authority to teach, to impose spiritual prohibitions, and to judge, he did not abandon his rule, remembering the words of the Lord: “Whoever wishes to be the foremost, let him be the least of all and the servant of all.” Remembering this instruction of the Savior, he humbled himself and considered himself the least of all, and set an example for all: he was first in all labors and came earlier than all to church singing, never leaning against the wall. And therefore that place prospered, and the number of the brethren increased. If after Compline or late at night, going around the cells of his brethren, he heard that someone was praying, or occupied with some handiwork, or reading the Divine Scriptures, or lamenting and grieving over his sins, he rejoiced over these and prayed to God for them, that they might have strength to complete their good undertakings to the end. But if he heard two or three gathered together laughing, he was displeased, and, knocking on the little window, by his unseen visit he ended their idle conversations. And in the morning, summoning them to himself, he did not rebuke them with fury and harshness, but quietly, meekly, and mercifully spoke with them, teaching them to be calm and attentive, and to pray about their sins in silence and stillness. And he instructed them mercifully and with long-suffering, as it is said: “The righteous shall show me mercy and rebuke me.” On the disobedient, who did not understand their fault and persisted in it, he imposed a penance, having set them on the path of correction: to live as monks, in the fear of God, to pray about their sins with compunction and silence, and not to converse after the mephimon, and not to go from cell to cell except to the abbot.

On the Establishment of the Common Life

Then Greeks came from Constantinople to the monastery of Saint Sergius, sent by Philotheus, Patriarch of Constantinople, and they delivered to him the patriarch’s blessing, a cross and paramand, a schema, and a letter. The blessed one said to them: “Have you not made a mistake? Perhaps you were sent to another? I am unworthy to receive these gifts from the most holy patriarch.” They asked: “Are you Sergius who lives in Radonezh?” He answered: “I am the sinful monk Sergius.” They answered: “Philotheus, the Ecumenical Patriarch, blesses you.” The saint said nothing. After this he showed them great honors, and, having ordered them to remain in his monastery, he himself took the gifts and the patriarch’s letter, and went on foot to the blessed Metropolitan Alexis in the city of Moscow. The blessed Sergius, who loved to labor until late old age, never rode a horse, but always walked on foot.

Having come to the city to the blessed Metropolitan Alexis, he gave him the gifts and the patriarchal letter and told him what he had heard from those sent.
