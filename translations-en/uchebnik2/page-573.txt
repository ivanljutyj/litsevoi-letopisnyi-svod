He was of good parents, of good stock. He grew as a good shoot springs from a good root, taking into himself all the virtues of that root. From his earliest years he was like a noble garden, flourishing like a precious fruit. This youth was handsome and well-behaved. As he grew, he prospered only in good deeds. But worldly beauty did not entice him, and all the vanity of the world was empty to him. One might say that he wished to despise and overcome nature itself, often whispering the words of David: “What profit is there in my blood, when I go down to corruption?” Night and day, without ceasing, he prayed to God, Who has always been the advocate of such ascetics and helped them to be saved. How can one tell of his other virtues: his calm, meekness, love of silence, humility, freedom from anger, simplicity without guile? He bore equal love for all people, never grew angry, never contradicted anyone, never took offense, never showed weakness, never laughed; and if he wanted to smile—and this did happen—he did so chastely and with restraint. He always walked sorrowful, with a mournful countenance. Very often he wept, tears flowing down his cheeks, signifying our pitiful and sorrowful life. The words of the Psalter were always on his lips, and he was adorned with temperance in all things. He rejoiced in curbing bodily desires; he wore simple clothing worthily, and this gladdened him more than fine clothes, vain and swiftly passing display. He never tasted beer or mead, nor even breathed their smell. He loved the fasting life more than the sweet and abundant. In the Gospel the Lord says: “Take heed lest your hearts be weighed down with surfeiting and drunkenness”; in another place it is said: “Martha, Martha, why are you anxious and troubled about many things? But one thing is needful; Mary has chosen the good part, which shall not be taken away from her”; in another place it is said: “Woe to you who are full, for you shall hunger; woe to you who laugh now, for you shall mourn and weep.”

The Apostle Paul says: “Food does not commend us to God.” Basil the Great says: “If you wish to enter paradise, restrain your belly, flee drunkenness.” And the Lord says: “Enter by the narrow gate; for wide and spacious is the gate that leads to destruction, and many are those who enter by it; for narrow is the gate and hard the way that leads to life, and few there are who find it.” This blessed one himself lived a harsh life: always in fasting, in labors, grieving, sorrowing, and in compunction, in constant chastity.

His brothers Stephen and Peter, the sons of Cyril, married. But the third son of his parents, this blessed youth Bartholomew, did not wish to marry, but ardently desired to be tonsured a monk, and thereby to please God and serve Him with all his soul day and night, according to what is said: “It is better to cling to the house of the Lord than to live in the villages of sinners.”

And he began to ask his parents to let him be tonsured a monk. They answered him: “Child, bear with us a little in hardship...”
