He himself did not want to accept the tsardom and hid from them, having put on black robes.

Then they took his wife, brought her to the great church of St Sophia, and for a long time compelled her: “Tell us, where is your husband?” But she said nothing to them about her husband.

Afterward they took a man named Nicholas, a soldier, and placed a crown on him without [the blessing of] the patriarch, and they remained with him in the church of St Sophia for six days and six nights.

Tsar Isaakovich was in Blachernae and wished secretly to bring the Latins and the boyars into the city, but the boyars entreated the tsar and did not let him admit the Latins into the city, saying: “We are with you.”

Then, frightened by the arrival of the Latins, the boyars conspired with Murtzuphlus, seized Tsar Isaakovich, and placed the crown on Murtzuphlus; but Murtzuphlus released Isaakovich from prison and promised not to depose Isaakovich from the tsardom, but to rule according to his will.

Murtzuphlus sent to Nicholas and to the people in St Sophia, saying: “I have captured your enemy Isaakovich; I am your tsar, and to Nicholas I give the first place among the boyars.” Nicholas wished to lay aside the crown, but all the people not only did not allow him to lay aside the crown, but moreover swore: “Whoever forsakes Nicholas will be cursed.”

That same day, when night fell, all fled, and Nicholas and his wife were taken captive; Murtzuphlus imprisoned them, and he confined Alexios Isaakovich in prison.

Murtzuphlus himself became tsar, hoping to defeat the Latins; but the Latins, learning that Isaakovich had been taken captive, began battle around Constantinople and asked Murtzuphlus to release Isaakovich to them: “We will return to the German Tsar and to the Pope of Rome, by whom we were sent, and his tsardom will remain to you.”

But Murtzuphlus and all the boyars did not spare his life and put Isaakovich to death, saying to the Latins: “He is dead; come and see for yourselves.” Then the Latins were grieved by the crime that had been committed, since the German Tsar and the Pope of Rome had not instructed them to do harm to Tsargrad, and they said to themselves: “If Isaakovich, with whom we came here, is not with us, it is better for us to die at the walls of Tsargrad than to withdraw in fear and shame.”

And they began to prepare for battle against Tsargrad, and decided, as before, to use rams on the masts on the ships; on other ships they prepared siege engines and ladders; and on others they decided to lower barrels filled with pitch and tow over the railings, and, setting them alight, throw them toward the city and the houses in order to set the city on fire.
