Later Yaropolk, Prince of Kiev, made peace with Vsevolod, giving Pereyaslavl to his brother Andrei and Vladimir to Izyaslav Mstislavich. In the same year Michael, Metropolitan of Kiev and All Rus, sent an interdict upon all Novgorod.

The Novgorodians, with their prince Vsevolod Mstislavich and their bishop Nifont, were greatly grieved, and they sent the hegumen Isaiah to Metropolitan Michael in Kiev with a petition, asking forgiveness, together with the Novgorod envoy Yakun Ivankov.

Metropolitan Michael of Kiev and All Rus was received by all Novgorod with great honor and joy. And again certain evil men began to incite Vsevolod Mstislavich to make war on Suzdal and Rostov.

Hearing this, Michael, Metropolitan of Kiev and All Rus, forbade Prince Vsevolod Mstislavich and all the Novgorodians to shed Christian blood in vain or to make war. Metropolitan Michael wished to go to Kiev, but the Novgorodians would not let him go, nor any one of his men, so that no news might reach Suzdal and Rostov. The metropolitan then began to say to them: “If you go to Suzdal and Rostov without heeding me, it will go ill for you; for though I am sinful, God will hear even me, a sinner, as I pray to Him with tears.” But they began to accuse the Suzdalians and Rostovians of many offenses. In the same year, in winter, Prince Vsevolod Mstislavich set out with an army, with the Germans and with all the Novgorod forces, against Suzdal and Rostov. Their way was very hard, for evil days had come, with strong winds and very terrible whirlwinds.

The Suzdalians and Rostovians met them at Zhdane Hill, and there was fierce fighting there at Zhdane Hill, and much harm was done to the Novgorodians. They killed a glorious man, their posadnik Ivanko Dankov, and Zhiryata Yakunov, and Petrilo Nikulin, and Dansha Yakshich, and killed many good and brave Novgorodian men, and much Christian blood was shed.

The Novgorodians fled with their prince to Novgorod; and when they came to Novgorod, they began to denounce Prince Vsevolod Mstislavich over the Suzdal war; and he planned to flee to the Germans.

The Novgorodians seized him and put him under guard, and kept him under guard for two months and four days with his wife, his children, and his mother-in-law; and a hundred armed men guarded him closely every day and every night. And these were the charges brought against him: why he did not protect the common people; why he wished to rule in Pereyaslavl; why he wished to go against the Suzdalians and Rostovians; why, having gone, he did not fight stoutly; why he fled before all others; and why he loved to play and amuse himself, and not the people—
