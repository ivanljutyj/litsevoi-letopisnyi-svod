It is impossible to recount with what spiritual labours and with what cares he had to contend when he began to live in solitude, manfully remaining for a sufficiently long time in the desert forest.
His steadfast and most holy soul undoubtedly endured much, being
in solitude, without a single person near, fulfilling the statutes and rules of monastic life blamelessly, without stumbling and irreproachably. What
mind, or what tongue could tell of the beginning of his zeal and of his love
for God, of the secret virtue of his struggle; or describe his solitude and
his bold ventures, his groanings and supplications, and the ceaseless prayers with which
he constantly turned to God, his fervent tears, spiritual weeping, heartfelt sighs, all-night vigils, fervent singing, unceasing prayers, standing without rest, diligent reading, fasting, thirst, lying on the ground, spiritual poverty, scarcity and want in all things? And
how much he suffered from demons and beasts! Demons turned into both
serpents and reptiles and beasts, into demonic visions.

But he protected himself with prayers, and divine power guarded him.

Many beasts came to him: wolves and bears. And one bear
came to him very often. And the saint understood that it did not come in
anger, but for food, and desiring to comfort the saint in his sorrows. The saint brought out
bread from his cell and laid it on a log and on stumps. The bear came, quiet
and gentle, and joyfully and gladly began to look at the saint. The saint gave thanks to God,
understanding that this fierce beast, the bear, had been sent by God
to comfort him, like a sheep settled with him in the wilderness. And he fed it
every day, leaving bread on the log or on a stump, and the bear ate it. And when very little bread remained for the saint, he fed the bear
and remained hungry himself. He was ready to leave himself hungry rather than
leave the bear, his wilderness companion, hungry. The saint loved silence very much, and therefore he dared to settle in a deserted place,
and lived there alone, in silence, because, having tasted the divine sweetness
of silence, he could no longer turn back and abandon the chosen path.

And so he lived there in solitude, in silence and stillness for two or
three years. And after this, monks who had learned of him, living in the vicinity,
began to come to him. First one by one, then two by two, sometimes three by three,
and they asked him: “Father, receive us; we wish to live with you in this place
and save our souls.” But the saint, not wishing to violate the sweetness of silence
and stillness, did not want to receive them, knowing also that the wilderness life was
hard and harsh. People came to him with joy, and afterwards went away from him displeased,
