He labored in his righteous works. And he did not hand this work to anyone, although many of the brethren wanted to bake prosphora. But the venerable one did everything himself: he himself cooked kutia, made candles, and composed canons. Although he had accepted the abbacy and the authority to teach, to impose spiritual prohibition, and to decide, he did not abandon his rule, remembering the words of the Lord: “Whoever wishes to be the greatest, let him be the least of all and the servant of all.” Remembering this teaching of the Savior, he humbled himself and considered himself lesser than all, and set an example for all: he was first in all labors and came to church singing before everyone, never leaning against a wall. And therefore that place prospered, and the number of the brethren increased. If after Compline or late at night, going around the cells of his brethren, he heard someone praying, or engaged in some handiwork, or reading the Divine Scripture, or someone weeping and lamenting over his sins, he rejoiced for them and prayed to God for them, that they might have strength to complete their good beginnings to the end. If he heard two or three gathered together laughing, he was displeased, and, knocking at the window, by his invisible visit he stopped their idle conversations. And in the morning, summoning them to himself, he did not reprove them with fury and harshness, but quietly, meekly, and mercifully spoke with them, teaching them to be calm and attentive, and to pray for their sins in silence and stillness. And he taught them mercifully and with long-suffering, as it is said: “The righteous will show me mercy and reprove me.” On the disobedient, who did not understand their fault and persisted in it, he imposed a penance, having set them on the path of correction: to live in monastic fashion, with the fear of God, to pray for their sins with compunction and silence, and after the mephimon not to converse, and not to go from cell to cell except to the abbot.

On the Establishment of the Common Life

Then Greeks came from Constantinople to St. Sergius at the monastery, sent by Philotheus, Patriarch of Constantinople, and they gave him the patriarch’s blessing, a cross and a paramand, a schema, and a letter. The blessed one said to them: “Have you not erred? Perhaps you were sent to another? I am unworthy to receive these gifts from the most holy patriarch.” They asked: “Are you Sergius, who lives in Radonezh?” He answered: “I am the sinful monk Sergius.” They said: “Philotheus, the Ecumenical Patriarch, blesses you.” The saint said nothing. After this he showed them great honor, and, having ordered them to remain in his monastery, he himself took the gifts and the patriarch’s letter, and went on foot to the blessed Metropolitan Alexis in the city of Moscow. Blessed Sergius, who loved to labor until late old age, never rode a horse, but always walked on foot.

Having come to the city to the blessed Metropolitan Alexis, he gave him the gifts and the patriarch’s letter and told him what he had heard from those sent.
