So that they could not see one another, who was fighting whom, and many died from the smell of powder. And they fought on the walls until the darkness of night separated them.

The Turks nevertheless withdrew to their camps, leaving their dead; and the townspeople fell like dead men, leaving only guards on the walls.

In the morning the tsar ordered the corpses of the dead to be gathered and buried; the number of slain glorious and renowned Greeks was 1,740, and of Franks and Armenians, 700.

The tsar with his boyars went along the city walls, wishing to see the warriors, and not a sound was to be heard, for all were sleeping after their labor. And he saw the moats full of corpses, and others in the channels, and on the banks heaps of dead, and they counted eighteen thousand slain Turks.

And the tsar ordered all their siege engines and devices to be given to the fire. And the tsar himself went with the patriarch and the hierarchs and the whole sacred council into the holy and Great Church to offer prayers and thanksgiving to almighty God and the most pure Mother of God, hoping that the godless one had withdrawn when he saw such destruction of his warriors. But he, being beast-like, thought otherwise. On the second day he sent to look upon his dead. And when they told him the number of the dead, he sent many regiments to take away their corpses.

But the tsar forbade it, saying that they would not engage them in battle, but let them clear the moats and channels; and taking their corpses, let them burn them without battle.

The godless Mehmed saw that he had gained nothing but had only destroyed his own, and he ordered his engineers soon to add many cannons and guns and to prepare siege engines; and only on the seventh day did the infidel order the army to go to the city and fight as at first, without respite.

Tsar Constantine sent by sea and by land to Morea to his brothers, and to Venice and Genoa, asking for help; but his brothers did not succeed, because there was great strife among them, and they were at war with the Albanians; and the Franks did not wish to give help, reasoning:

“We shall not help them; let the Turks take it, and from them we shall take Constantinople”; and therefore there was no help from anywhere.

Only one Genoese lord named Giustiniani came to the tsar’s aid on two ships and two armed galleys, having with him 600 brave men, and, passing through the whole naval host of the Turkish sultan, he reached the wall of Constantinople safe and sound.

Seeing him, the tsar rejoiced greatly and showed him great honor, since he was known to the tsar. And he asked the tsar where the weakest place in the city was for an assault.
