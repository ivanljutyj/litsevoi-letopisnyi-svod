He went from Pskov to the Germans for the Eighth Council, and the Germans escorted him with honors. When he came to the German land, to the city of Yuryev, the Orthodox Christians living there and all the priests of the Greek law met him with crosses; the Latins and all the Germans also met him with crosses according to their Roman law.

But Isidore, having violated the oath he had given to Grand Prince Vasily Vasilyevich to preserve piety, when he arrived, bowed and crossed himself, and kissed the cross of the Roman law, and escorted the cross of the Roman law, and then approached the holy crosses of the Greek law; and after escorting the cross of the Roman law to the kostel, that is, to their church, he paid it honor, and escorted it more than the cross of the Greek law; he did not honor the Greek law so.

On Abraham, Bishop of Suzdal

God-loving Abraham, Bishop of Suzdal, and all Orthodox Christians who were with the metropolitan on that journey saw this, and were all seized with fear that, before even being at the council, he was acting thus.

In the same spring, on Palm Saturday, Kolomna burned, and little remained of it; the town caught fire at vespers, and by sunset the fire ceased, and people began to walk over the burned site.

In the same year the Tatars conquered Ryazan, did much evil, and went back to their own land. In the same year the Tatars took Lithuania captive and returned to their own land with many captives.

In the same year the Lithuanian prince Yury Semenovich came to Novgorod.

In the same year in Novgorod they raised on the old foundation the fallen stone church of St Nicholas at Vezhishchi.

In the year 6947 from Adam (the year 1447 from Christ).
On Metropolitan Isidore

Isidore the Greek, Metropolitan of Kiev and all Rus, arrived in the Roman region, in the city called Ferrara, to Pope Eugene of Rome for the false Eighth Council. There the metropolitan met the Greek emperor John Manuilovich of Constantinople, and with him the ecumenical patriarch Joseph; with them were the metropolitan of the Trapezuntine king, the metropolitan of the Iberian king, and metropolitans of many other lands; there were also bishops, 120 in number, and many learned men, and great monastic elders, and many philosophers, and simple Greek elders, and laymen, and a multitude of Greeks; also archbishops, bishops, and monks of the Latin lands, and Aquitanians, Latins, Germans, Italians, Carinthians, French, Berbers, and Caffians—a countless multitude of them gathered.
