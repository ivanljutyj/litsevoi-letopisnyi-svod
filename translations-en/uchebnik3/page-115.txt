The emperor, drawing his sword, turned against the Turks, and when his sword reached anyone on the shoulder or ribs, he cut him in two. The Turks, terrified by the emperor’s strength, fled from the city; and the commanders, soldiers, and all the people, seeing the bravery and courage of their emperor, all galloped against the Turks and drove them back beyond the ditches.

Mehmed stood firm and ordered the Turks to fight and turn back against the Greeks; and the battle was very dark, because their arrows darkened the light. The Greeks from both sides of the wall threw hot pitch at the Turks, setting alight large bundles of pitch.

The sun had already set, but the battle did not cease. Yet the godless one set countless fires everywhere. He himself galloped about all the places, shouting and crying out, urging on his men, hoping soon to destroy the city.

But the Greeks and other people who were on the walls, arming themselves with boldness, cried to one another: “Let us hasten, brothers, to the place appointed for us and die for the holy churches!” And the battle with the Turks was fierce until midnight.

And they drove them from the wall and from the city down to the ground. The battle ended, but they did not withdraw from the city, for the accursed one himself stood, guarding his fortifications and other devices, not allowing the townspeople to set fire to them.

On the 27th day of May the infidel ordered the city to be bombarded near the ruined place with many cannons and handguns, and at the ninth hour of the day, aiming a great cannon, they struck the tower, and a second time, and a third, and smashed the tower. Thus that day passed.

Night fell; Justinian with all his company and all the Franks began to rebuild the tower; and a stone cannonball came flying at the end of its flight and struck Justinian in the chest; he fell to the ground, and scarcely reviving him, they carried him home.

The boyars and all the Franks who were with him were dismayed and did not know what to do. This was God’s will for the destruction of the city, because he guarded this place with the breach in it with great strength and courage, for he was brave and wise and very skilled in the art of war.

When they told the emperor, he was greatly grieved and quickly set out to him with all the nobles and physicians, comforting him. The physicians strove all that night to help him, and when they had healed his chest, the place bruised by the blow, he took some rest from his illness; they gave him a little food to taste and drink, and he slept a little that night. But the rest of his company did not have time to rebuild the tower.

Justinian ordered himself carried there, and they began to build the tower with great zeal on 28 May.

The Turks saw those building the tower, and soon a multitude of them rushed upon the ruined place; the Greeks also came against them, and there was a great battle.
